Elicit does two jobs: it searches the academic literature for you, and it reads and summarises papers. For the search half, the alternatives are other online tools like Consensus and Semantic Scholar, because finding papers needs a big online database. For the reading half, you don't need the cloud at all: an offline app like GreenCube reads the PDFs you already have on your own PC, so unpublished work never gets uploaded.

I build GreenCube, so weigh my opinion with that in mind. Here's how the options split, and when keeping your documents offline matters.

What Elicit does

Elicit is an online research assistant. You ask a research question, it finds relevant papers, summarises them and pulls details (methods, sample sizes, results) into a table you can compare. It's free to start, with paid plans for heavier use. Everything you upload or type is processed on its servers.

That's fine for published papers. It's a harder question for your own draft chapter, interview transcripts or a client's report. Many universities ask researchers not to put unpublished data or participant information into commercial AI tools, so check your own institution's policy before you upload anything.

Online alternatives for finding papers

  • Consensus searches published research and answers your question with the papers it drew on.
  • Semantic Scholar is a free paper search engine from the Allen Institute for AI, with short summaries of many papers.
  • NotebookLM from Google answers questions from the sources you upload. It's good at sticking to your sources, but it runs in Google's cloud.
  • Perplexity searches the web and cites what it finds; it covers far more than academic work, so check that what it cites is a real study.

None of these work offline, and none can, because the papers live on their servers. There's a longer list in AI for researchers.

An offline alternative for reading your own documents

Once you have the PDFs, an AI on your own computer can do much of the reading work: summarise a paper, explain a method, answer questions about a document, turn notes into flashcards or a quiz. Nothing leaves your PC, so it works for drafts and data you can't upload.

GreenCube is a Windows app that does this. It picks the AI model for your PC (Cube 2B on smaller PCs, Cube 4B on PCs with a graphics card, both built on Google's Gemma), and it reads PDFs, Word files and photos of pages. Chats and files are saved on your PC. It needs the internet once, to sign in (only to check you paid; it never sees your chats or files) and to download the AI, and after that it works offline. It costs €9.99 once, with a 15-day refund.

Be clear about what it isn't. GreenCube won't find papers for you, and it isn't built to extract data from hundreds of studies into one table the way Elicit is. It works with the files you give it.

If you'd rather go free, apps like GPT4All and AnythingLLM can chat with a folder of documents on your PC, with more setup. See AnythingLLM alternatives and private PDF AI.

How to choose a document AI

  1. Find out where the reading happens. Does the app send your document to a server, or only contact one for licensing and downloads?
  2. Match it to your files. Typed PDFs are easy for any tool. Scanned pages and photos need a model that can read images.
  3. Test it on a paper you know well. Ask three questions you already know the answers to and check them. Every AI, cloud or local, can state something wrong with confidence.
  4. Check the price terms: a subscription, a free tool, or a one-time price with a refund.

What your PC needs

A laptop with about 8 GB of RAM can run a small model and read documents; it's just slower. Here's how fast GreenCube wrote on the PCs we tested: 5–7 tokens a second on an 8 GB laptop with no graphics card (about reading speed), 14–18 on a 16 GB laptop, and 37–76 on most gaming PCs with a 6 GB or bigger card. Long PDFs take longer than short chats on any machine. To check yours, try Can my PC run AI?

When the cloud is still the better choice

For a systematic search across thousands of papers, use an online tool; that's what they're for. The big cloud AIs are also smarter than anything that fits on a laptop on hard reasoning. A sensible way to split it: search and screen published work online, and do the reading, note-taking and drafting on your own unpublished material offline.
