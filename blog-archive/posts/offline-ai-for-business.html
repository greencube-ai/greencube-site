Yes, a business can run AI offline so that confidential documents are processed on its own computers instead of a vendor's servers. That removes one real risk, but it doesn't remove the need for governance: you still need to know which models you run, who can use them, how they're updated and what happens when a laptop is lost. The safest way in is a small pilot on one low-risk task, run through a short checklist like the one below.

I build GreenCube, an offline AI app for Windows, so I'm not neutral. The checklist works the same whichever tool you choose.

What offline AI means for a business

Offline AI, or local inference, means the model runs on a device or server you control. A contract you ask it to summarise is read on that machine, with no trip to a provider's data centre. There are four common setups:

  • a desktop app on each employee's laptop, simple and single-user;
  • an on-premises server that several staff use over the internal network, which needs more IT work;
  • an air-gapped system with no network at all, for defence or highly regulated research (see air-gapped AI);
  • a hybrid, where daily work runs locally and selected tasks go to a cloud model under clear rules.

The trade-off is capability against hardware. Models small enough for an ordinary laptop are good at summarising, drafting and answering questions about documents. They're weaker than the big cloud models on complex, multi-step reasoning, and slower on older machines.

Where it fits and where it doesn't

Good fits are tasks where the data is sensitive and the job is well defined: reviewing and summarising contracts, HR or client files, drafting reports from internal notes, and work in places with poor internet, such as site visits or client offices.

Poor fits are tasks that need scale or the strongest reasoning: customer chat across thousands of conversations at once, large-scale analytics, or hard problems where the quality of a frontier model really matters. Those usually belong in the cloud, with a proper contract and data agreement. Offline AI vs cloud AI covers that choice in more depth.

The 8-step pilot checklist

  1. Classify your data. Decide which documents are fine for a pilot and which need stricter handling later.
  2. Pick one low-risk, repetitive task. Summarising internal meeting notes is a better start than your most sensitive process.
  3. Match the model and hardware to the task. Plain text summaries need less than scanned documents and images. Check what your laptops can run before you buy anything.
  4. Test on real documents. Use a representative set, not the easy examples, and keep a few aside so you can rerun the same test after every update.
  5. Check what goes over the network. Run the tool with the Wi-Fi off, then look at the firewall log while it's online. You should be able to explain every connection, such as licence checks or updates.
  6. Set encryption and access. Full-disk encryption on every device (BitLocker on Windows), a password per user, and the same access rules you apply to the files themselves.
  7. Decide on retention. How long chats and generated files are kept, where, and who can see them.
  8. Define success and a human check. Measure something concrete, like time saved per document, and have a person review a sample of outputs before anyone relies on them.

Run the pilot with the three to five people who will use the tool every day and ask them to flag anything that looks wrong. Expand only once the pilot has passed all eight steps.

What NIST says still applies

The US National Institute of Standards and Technology published a Generative AI Profile for its AI Risk Management Framework. Its point, put simply, is that you remain responsible for an AI system wherever it runs. For an offline deployment, that translates into a few practical controls:

  • an inventory of which models you run, their versions and the machines they're on;
  • a record of where each model file came from;
  • access controls on who can use the tool and export its results;
  • logs good enough to investigate a problem;
  • a plan for a lost, stolen or compromised device;
  • a way to retire old models and wipe their data.

In practice, device security often matters more than which model you pick. A stolen laptop with an unencrypted disk undoes the privacy benefit no matter how the AI behaves. Offline AI security walks through the device steps.

And offline operation doesn't make you compliant with any law by itself. If you're in a regulated sector, confirm your obligations with whoever owns compliance. For Europe, GDPR-compliant AI is a good place to start.

Keeping it up to date

With the cloud gone, so are automatic updates on the provider's side. Someone on your team now owns them. Get new model files and app versions only from the official source, check signatures or checksums, update on a set schedule and write down what changed. Keep the previous working version so a bad update doesn't leave staff without a tool. When a device is retired, wipe the models and any saved chats along with everything else. On one laptop this is light work; across a department or a shared server it grows quickly, so weigh it before you scale.

Helping staff adopt it

Resistance usually comes from unclear expectations, not from the technology. Tell people plainly what the tool is good at, what always needs a human check, and what it must never be used for. A half-hour session where staff try it on their own documents does more than a long manual. Present it as help with tedious tasks, not a replacement for their judgement, and keep a feedback channel open for the first few weeks.

GreenCube as an example

GreenCube shows what the single-laptop setup looks like. It's a Windows app that checks the PC and picks the model: Cube 2B on smaller machines, Cube 4B on PCs with a graphics card, both built on Google's Gemma. It reads PDFs, Word files and photos, and makes documents, slide decks and summaries. Chats and files are saved on the PC.

Against the checklist: it goes online to sign in (only to check the licence was paid), to download the AI on first launch, to check for updates, and for web lookups only if a user switches Online on. Updates are signed and checked by the app before installing. It doesn't encrypt the disk or manage users, so steps 6 and 7 are still yours, and it has no compliance certification. It's a desktop app for individual users, not a shared server, and it isn't as strong as the big cloud models on complex reasoning. It costs €9.99 (US$9.99) once, with a 15-day refund, which makes it cheap to try in a pilot before deciding anything bigger.
