Offline AI runs on your own computer: you download the model once and it answers with the Wi-Fi off. Cloud AI, like ChatGPT, Claude or Gemini, runs on the provider's servers and needs a connection for every message. Cloud is smarter on the hardest problems and knows today's news. Offline keeps your chats on your own computer, works anywhere and has no monthly bill. Most people are best served by using both, each for the jobs it does well.

I build GreenCube, an offline AI app, so weigh my opinion with that in mind. I'll try to be fair to both sides, because the honest answer really does depend on the task.

How the two differ

With cloud AI, your message travels to a data centre, a very large model writes the answer there, and the text comes back. The model never lives on your device. You pay with a subscription, per use through an API, or with limits on a free plan.

With offline AI (also called local or on-device AI), the model file sits on your disk and your own processor and graphics card do the work. Nothing has to go anywhere for the AI to answer. The catch is that the model has to fit on a normal computer, so it is much smaller than the ones in the cloud.

IBM describes it as a spectrum rather than a choice of one or the other, and that matches what I see. Plenty of people run some tasks locally and send others to the cloud. If you want the mechanics, how local AI works explains them in plain words.

When offline AI is the better pick

The clearest case is private material. Contracts, medical notes, HR files, an unpublished manuscript, your own diary: if you'd rather they never sat on someone else's server, a local model reads them on your machine. You don't have to read a provider's retention policy, because there's no provider holding your chats.

The second case is no connection. On a plane, on a train through tunnels, at a remote site or in a library with bad Wi-Fi, a cloud AI simply stops. A local one keeps going.

The third is steady, heavy use. Cloud plans have message caps, file limits or per-token bills that grow with use (see ChatGPT's free limits). A local model costs nothing extra per message, so you can summarise fifty PDFs in a row without watching a meter.

When cloud AI is the better pick

Hard reasoning is the big one. Long chains of maths, tricky code, subtle writing and very niche facts are where the large cloud models are clearly ahead. A model small enough for a laptop is good for study, writing and everyday questions, but it is not as clever.

Cloud AI also knows recent events when it can search the web, while a local model only knows what it learned before release. Very long inputs, like a whole codebase or a book-length report in one go, are easier in the cloud too, since a laptop has limited memory for long context.

And if you use AI once a week, or your computer is old and slow, a free cloud plan may just be the practical choice. A slow PC runs a local model slowly, and no app can change that.

Privacy and compliance

Running AI locally removes one big question: what a cloud provider does with your prompts and files. It does not make you compliant with HIPAA or GDPR on its own. You still need to protect the computer itself (disk encryption such as BitLocker, a password, separate user accounts if the PC is shared) and follow whatever rules apply to your data.

If you do use a cloud service for sensitive work, check whether your chats are used for training, how long they are kept, and, for health data in the US, whether the provider will sign a Business Associate Agreement. Is ChatGPT private? goes through this for ChatGPT, and GDPR-compliant AI covers the European side.

The tools people actually use

For running models locally, most people start with Ollama or LM Studio. Ollama started on the command line and serves models to other apps; LM Studio gives you a window where you browse and download models. Both are free, and both run model files in the GGUF format made popular by llama.cpp, the open-source engine underneath much of local AI. You pick the model yourself, which is great if you enjoy that and a lot of reading if you don't.

Developers who want both worlds sometimes build a router: requests go to a local model by default and only the hard ones, or the ones that need fresh information, go to a cloud API. That works well, but the routing rules are where private data can slip out, so they need testing.

Where GreenCube fits

GreenCube is the offline side, made for people who don't want to set anything up. It checks your PC and picks the model: Cube 2B on smaller PCs, Cube 4B on PCs with a graphics card, both built on Google's Gemma. Besides chat it makes slide decks, documents, flashcards, quizzes and images, and it reads your PDFs and photos. Your chats and files are saved on your PC.

It needs the internet once, to sign in (only to check you paid) and to download the AI on first launch. After that it works offline, and it only goes online for updates or if you switch Online on for web lookups. It's for Windows and costs €9.99 once. It won't beat ChatGPT on the hardest problems, which is why I'd say the sensible setup for most people is both: cloud when you're online and need the strongest model, offline for private work and for the times the connection isn't there.
