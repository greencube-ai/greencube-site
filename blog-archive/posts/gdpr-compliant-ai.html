"GDPR compliant AI" isn't a property a tool can have on its own. It describes how an organisation uses AI with personal data: a lawful basis for each use, privacy designed in from the start, people told what happens to their data and able to use their rights, and records that show all of this. In 2026 there's a second layer, the EU AI Act, for some kinds of AI system. Where the AI runs matters too: if it runs on your own computers, the personal data you put into it isn't sent to another company, which removes a whole set of questions.

This is an overview, not legal advice. Your data protection officer or lawyer decides what applies to you. I build GreenCube, an AI app that runs offline, so read the last section with that in mind.

How GDPR principles apply to AI

The GDPR was written before large language models, but its principles fit them surprisingly well. They apply at every stage where personal data is involved: collecting data, training a model, using it, and storing what it produces.

A few consequences are easy to miss. Purpose limitation means you can't reuse HR records to train a customer service bot just because you have them. Data minimisation means feeding the AI only what the task needs. Accuracy matters because a model can produce confident, wrong statements about a real person. And data protection by design means privacy-friendly settings are the default, not an option someone has to find.

Practical steps

The most common failure is treating the lawful basis as a box to tick. If you rely on legitimate interest, the European Data Protection Board's opinion on AI models describes the test: the interest must be legitimate, the processing necessary for it, and the interest not overridden by people's rights. Write that balancing down. The same opinion warns that a model built on unlawfully processed data can affect whether you may lawfully use it, so ask vendors where their training data came from.

Do a data protection impact assessment early, before personal data enters the system. It's required when processing is likely to be high risk, and AI on personal data often is. If the AI Act also applies to your system, it makes sense to cover both in one piece of work.

Watch the prompts. People paste personal data into AI tools without thinking about it, so decide what may go in, and where you can, strip names and identifiers before text reaches the model. France's regulator, the CNIL, has published recommendations on AI and the GDPR that favour proportionate measures like this.

Check where data goes. The GDPR applies to EU residents' data wherever the provider is. If a cloud AI vendor processes it outside the EU, you need a valid transfer mechanism, such as standard contractual clauses, and a proper data processing agreement. Read those documents rather than relying on a "we're compliant" badge.

Keep your records of processing up to date, and decide what you log about AI use so you can show a regulator what happened if you ever need to.

What the EU AI Act adds

The AI Act sits on top of the GDPR. Its heaviest duties fall on systems it classes as high risk, such as AI used to screen job applicants, score credit or identify people by their biometrics. For those it asks for documented data governance including checks for bias, human oversight, technical documentation, a conformity assessment before launch, and monitoring afterwards.

Its obligations are being phased in over several years, and the timing for high-risk systems has been under discussion in Brussels, so check the current dates rather than relying on an article. Many everyday uses, like drafting emails or summarising documents, aren't high risk under the Act, but the GDPR still applies to any personal data in them.

Where the AI runs changes the picture

A lot of the work above comes from sending personal data to an outside AI provider: the processing agreement, the transfer mechanism, the vendor review. When the model runs on your own computer, the text you give it isn't sent to anyone, so for that step there's no outside processor and no international transfer to arrange.

It doesn't make anything compliant by itself. You're still the controller, you still need a lawful basis, the laptops still need securing, and people still have their rights over what you store. But it shrinks the list of questions.
