What a useful study guide includes

Not every summary is worth revising from. A guide that will actually help you on exam day has:

  • a short overview of what the chapter covers
  • a summary for each section, not one long block
  • 8 to 12 key terms with plain definitions
  • worked examples and application questions, if the subject involves solving problems
  • 10 to 20 practice questions, mixing recall and application, with the answers kept separate
  • a page number or quote for every claim that isn't common knowledge

The last point is the one people skip, and it matters most. A summary can read beautifully and still contain things that aren't in your PDF. If you can't trace a claim back to a page, you can't check it.

Step by step

  1. Prepare the file. Split it by exam scope, one chapter or unit per file, name it clearly ("Chapter 7 Cell Biology" beats "notes2"), and drop covers, contents pages and anything outside your test.
  2. If it's a scan or a photo of a textbook, check the AI has read it properly. Formulas, chart labels and diagram captions are where reading scans usually goes wrong, so compare those against the page.
  3. Ask for an outline that follows the document's own headings, two or three sentences per section, and a page reference after each main claim. "Summarise this" gets you something generic.
  4. Then ask for the active parts: the key terms, the practice questions, a one-page cheat sheet, and the answer key on its own so you're not tempted to peek.
  5. Save it. Print the cheat sheet, move the flashcards into whatever app you use, and keep a dated copy so you can tell what changed if you regenerate it.

If your course mixes slides, scanned readings and typed notes, put them in one file per exam topic in the order you were taught them. Page references only mean something if the order makes sense.

Catch the mistakes before they cost you marks

AI summaries can state things confidently that simply aren't in the source. It happens most with exactly what exams test: numbers, dates, formulas and names.

A quick check catches most of it. Ask the AI a handful of questions about the chapter, then look up each answer in the PDF yourself. Anything you can't find on the page, mark as uncertain or delete; don't learn it as fact. Keep the original PDF open next to the guide, not just while you make it but all the way to the exam.

Turn the guide into revision that works

A finished guide is half the job. What makes material stick is pulling it out of your memory again and again, spread over days, rather than rereading it. The research on this is consistent, and RetrievalPractice.org's guide to spacing explains it simply: you don't need a perfect timetable, and even loosely spaced reviews beat cramming.

  • Review flashcards in short daily sessions, weakest cards first.
  • Use the practice questions as practice, not as a check of what you already know, and mix questions from different sections.
  • Do one quiz a week covering everything so far, and in exam week revisit only what you're still getting wrong.

What an AI study guide can't do for you

It speeds up the grunt work of organising a dense PDF, but it doesn't do the learning. A neat outline feels like progress; the real progress happens when you close the guide and answer the questions from memory. Expect to fix formulas and dates, and to rewrite questions so they match your syllabus rather than a generic version of the topic.
