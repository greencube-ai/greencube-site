The best small LLM is the biggest one that fits comfortably in your computer's memory and does the task you need. As a rough guide: on a laptop with 8 GB of RAM, stay around 1 to 4 billion parameters (Gemma 4 E2B, Llama 3.2 3B, Qwen3 4B, Phi-4-mini). With 16 GB of RAM or a 6–8 GB graphics card, models up to about 8 billion become comfortable. Pick by your hardware first, then the task, then the licence, and only then look at leaderboards.

Leaderboards tell you which model scored best on a fixed test, on someone else's machine. They don't tell you whether it fits yours, and a model that doesn't fit is useless however well it scores. Here's how to choose, from the hardware up.

Which small LLM fits your hardware

Under 8 GB of RAM, no graphics card

Stick to models of about 1 to 3 billion parameters. They're fine for short chats, rewriting text, simple summaries and sorting or extracting information, but don't expect deep reasoning. Examples: Llama 3.2 1B and 3B, Qwen3 0.6B and 1.7B, and Meta's MobileLLM-Pro, a model of about 1 billion parameters that its model card says keeps near full quality at 4-bit and supports a 128k-token context.

8 to 16 GB of RAM, or a 6–8 GB graphics card

This is where most everyday local assistants live: models of about 3 to 8 billion parameters. They handle chat, document summaries, study help and light coding well. Good ones to try are Gemma 4 E2B and E4B, Microsoft's Phi-4-mini (3.8 billion parameters), Qwen3 4B and 8B, and Llama 3.1 8B. Longer answers are where a slower machine starts to show.

A 12–24 GB graphics card, or 32 GB of RAM

Now mid-sized models open up, around 12 to 30 billion parameters at 4-bit: Gemma 3 12B and 27B, Mistral Small (24B) and Qwen3 14B. They're noticeably better at coding, longer reasoning and following complicated instructions.

More than that

Workstations with 48 GB or more of graphics memory can run 70-billion-parameter models, long contexts and several models at once. At that point you're past "small".

What we measured

GreenCube, the app I build, runs Gemma 4 E2B on smaller PCs and Gemma 4 E4B on PCs with a graphics card (we call them Cube 2B and Cube 4B). On the PCs we tested, they wrote 5–7 tokens a second on an 8 GB laptop with no graphics card (about reading speed), 14–18 on a 16 GB laptop, and 37–76 on most gaming PCs with a 6 GB or bigger card. Those are the only models we've timed, so we don't give speeds for the others. The details are in Gemma 4 E2B vs E4B and best local AI models by PC.

How to choose a small LLM, step by step

  1. Check your memory. Look at your graphics card's VRAM in Task Manager (Performance, then GPU) and your RAM. The model file plus room for the conversation has to fit.
  2. Name the task. Chat, coding, documents and reading images favour different models. Only some small models can read images at all, so if you need that, pick one built for it, such as Gemma 4 or Gemma 3 4B and up.
  3. Read the licence. Qwen3, Gemma 4, Mistral Small and Phi-4-mini use permissive licences (Apache 2.0 or MIT). Llama uses Meta's own community licence, which has extra conditions. Read the actual licence file, not a summary.
  4. Check the language. If you work in Spanish, Hindi or anything other than English, check the model card; small models vary a lot here.
  5. Check the tooling. Is there a GGUF version for llama.cpp, LM Studio or Ollama? A great model with no file you can run is a dead end. See GGUF vs GGML.
  6. Test it with your own prompts: one long summary, one multi-turn chat, one task with a fixed format, and one hard question from your own field.

Quantization, in plain words

Models are trained with 16-bit numbers. Quantization stores them with fewer bits, so the file gets much smaller and the model runs faster, at a small cost in quality. Q4_K_M, a roughly 4-bit format, is the usual choice on home PCs and cuts the size by well over half. Q5_K_M and Q6_K are a little larger and a little closer to the original; use them if you have room to spare. More in LLM quantization.

Speed also depends on how long the conversation gets. The model keeps a memory of everything said so far (the KV cache), and it grows with every message, so a model that's quick on a short question can slow down on a 20-page document.

How to read benchmark scores

MMLU tests general knowledge across many school and university subjects. HumanEval checks whether the model writes code that passes small programming tests. MT-Bench scores multi-turn conversations, judged by another AI. WildBench uses real user conversations, which makes it closer to messy real use.

All of them are a first filter, not an answer. A model can score well on MMLU and still be poor at your contracts or your code style, and scores are only comparable when the models were tested the same way, including the same quantization. A slightly lower score at twice the speed is often the better choice for a chat.
