Use RAG (retrieval-augmented generation) when the model needs to know things: facts that change often, private documents, answers that must cite a source. Use fine-tuning when the model needs to behave differently: a fixed format, a consistent tone, a narrow task done the same way every time. If you're not sure which problem you have, start with plain prompts, add RAG for the knowledge gaps, and fine-tune only what's still wrong after that.

Below is what each one does, how they compare, and an afternoon's worth of measuring that tells you which you need.

What RAG is

RAG fetches relevant text at the moment a question is asked and puts it into the prompt, so the model answers from that text. The model's weights never change. The idea comes from a 2020 paper that paired a language model with a searchable index of documents (Lewis et al.).

Most of the work is in the retrieval, not the model. A typical pipeline:

  1. Split your documents into chunks, small enough to retrieve precisely but big enough to keep their meaning.
  2. Turn each chunk into a vector with an embedding model, and store the vectors in a vector database (or use a keyword index, or both).
  3. When a question comes in, find the closest chunks, optionally reorder them with a reranker, and pass the best few to the model with their sources.

Each extra step can improve the results and also adds time to every question, so measure both.

What fine-tuning is

Fine-tuning changes the model's weights by training it further on examples of what you want. Instead of telling the model how to answer each time, you teach it once. AWS's guidance makes the same split: fine-tuning suits tasks like summarisation or consistent output, while question answering over changing documents is better served by starting with RAG (AWS Prescriptive Guidance).

Full fine-tuning updates every weight and needs the most data and compute. Parameter-efficient methods are the usual choice now. LoRA trains small add-on matrices and leaves the original weights frozen; QLoRA does the same on top of a quantized base model, so it fits in much less memory. They cost far less, and you can keep several adapters for one base model. Full fine-tuning still has a place for deep changes across many tasks, but it's more prone to overfitting your examples.

RAG vs fine-tuning at a glance

Best forCurrent, private or citable knowledgeConsistent behaviour, tone or format

AWS's comparison notes that RAG can take in a new document in minutes, while fine-tuning takes hours to days depending on model size. For fast-changing knowledge, that alone usually decides it.

A decision checklist you can run in an afternoon

  1. Write a small test set, around 30 real prompts that include your hardest cases, and run them with plain prompting. That's your baseline.
  2. Read the failures and sort them: is the model missing knowledge, or behaving wrongly? Knowledge gaps point to RAG; format and tone problems point to fine-tuning.
  3. How often do the facts change? If weekly or daily, fine-tuning can't keep up.
  4. Must answers cite a source? RAG gives you that trail.
  5. Do you have good labelled examples? Without them, fine-tuning isn't an option yet.
  6. How tight is your latency budget? If retrieval makes answers too slow, fine-tuning avoids that step.
  7. Still unsure? Ship RAG, measure what's still failing, and fine-tune only that.

What to measure before you touch the weights

Retrieval and generation fail in different ways, so measure them separately. A decent overall score can hide a retrieval miss that the model is papering over with a plausible guess.

PartMeasureWhat it catches
RetrieverRecall@kThe right passage wasn't found at all
RerankerMean average precisionThe right passage was found but ranked too low
ModelGroundedness, or exact match against reference answersThe answer isn't supported by the retrieved text
Whole systemLatency and cost per questionWhether it's usable in practice

Keep every failed answer together with the passages it was given. Many "hallucinations" turn out to be retrieval misses. If you do fine-tune, rerun the same test set after every training run, since a retrain can quietly break something that used to work.

The staged workflow

  1. Ship the prompt-only baseline and score it.
  2. Add retrieval where it fails on missing or outdated knowledge, and score again.
  3. Look at what's left. If it's behaviour (wrong format, wrong tone, ignored instructions), that's a fine-tuning job.
  4. Fine-tune the smallest thing that fixes it, often a LoRA adapter or the embedding model used for retrieval, before considering a full retrain.

The two also combine well. In an AWS case study with Amazon Nova models, customising the model and adding RAG each improved answers, and using both together helped most, though results varied by dataset (AWS Machine Learning Blog).

When you need neither

If what you actually want is a private assistant that reads your own files, you may not need to build a pipeline at all. Local AI apps run a ready-made model on your computer and let you hand it documents directly; how local AI works explains the pieces. I build one, GreenCube, so weigh my opinion with that in mind: it reads your PDFs, Word files and photos on your PC, works offline after the first download, and costs €9.99 once for Windows. It won't match the big cloud models on the hardest problems, and it isn't a platform for building your own retrieval system. For a team that needs RAG over thousands of documents, the workflow above is still the way. For more on fitting long documents into a model, see context windows explained.
