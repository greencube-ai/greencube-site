The best Consensus alternative depends on the step of research you're on. For finding papers and pulling data out of them, Elicit is the closest match. For seeing how papers cite each other, try Scite or Research Rabbit. For understanding one paper at a time, SciSpace. And if your papers or notes are sensitive, analyse them in an app that runs on your own computer, like GreenCube, so they never leave it.

I build GreenCube, so weigh my opinion with that in mind. Plans and prices for research tools change often, so I describe what each one is for and leave out figures I can't check.

Cloud research tools

Elicit is built for structured literature searches: it finds papers and pulls their key details into tables you can compare. That makes it a good fit for systematic reviews. Like Consensus, it runs in the cloud (Elicit alternatives).

Scite is about citations. Instead of only counting them, it shows how later papers cite a study, which helps you see whether a finding has held up.

Research Rabbit maps the connections between papers as a graph, so you can start from one study and find the work around it. Useful early on, when you're still learning a field.

SciSpace focuses on understanding individual papers: you open one and ask questions about it. Consensus is more about answering a question across many papers, so pick by task.

Sourcely searches a large database of sources with detailed filters, which suits broad discovery.

Paperguide is another AI research assistant for finding and summarising papers. Try it next to Elicit and keep the one whose answers you trust more.

ChatGPT and Claude can summarise and explain papers you paste in, but they are general assistants, not search engines over the literature. Check any reference they give you, since they can produce citations that don't exist.

All of these send your search terms, and anything you upload, to their servers.

Keeping sensitive papers offline

Discovery needs the internet, because the paper indexes live online. Reading and analysing doesn't. If you're working with unpublished drafts, patient data or anything under a confidentiality agreement, you can split the work so only your search terms go online.

GreenCube is the app I make for that second half. It's a Windows app that runs the AI on your own PC: it reads PDFs, Word files and photos of pages, summarises them, answers questions about them, and makes study guides, flashcards and quizzes. It picks the model for your computer (Cube 2B on smaller PCs, Cube 4B on PCs with a graphics card, both built on Google's Gemma). Your files and chats are saved on your PC. It won't search the literature for you, and it's smaller than the big cloud models, so it's weaker on the hardest reasoning. On a slow laptop it's slow (on the PCs we tested, 5 to 7 tokens a second on an 8 GB laptop with no graphics card).

A simple private workflow

  1. Search in a discovery tool or a free index and note the titles and DOIs.
  2. Get the PDFs through your library or open repositories and keep them in one folder.
  3. Open them in a local app and make summaries and notes there.
  4. Build a short table: each claim, its source, and how strong the evidence looks.
  5. Compare the table with your research question and note what's missing.

If a paper's key results are in figures, check that whatever you use can read images. There's more on this in AI for researchers and making a study guide from a PDF.

No tool weighs the evidence for you

"Consensus" can sound like the tool has counted votes across science. In practice these tools search, summarise and map citations; the judgement is still yours. Before you cite anything, read the original paper. Free indexes like PubMed (biomedical and life sciences), Semantic Scholar (all fields) and IEEE Xplore (engineering and computing) are good places to check what a summary claims.

My honest view: cloud tools are better for scanning a whole field quickly. A local app is better once you have the papers and want to work with them privately. Many researchers will want one of each.
