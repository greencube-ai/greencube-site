A good school AI policy says plainly what students and staff may use AI for, at each age, and what they must disclose. It has five parts: purpose and scope, definitions, acceptable use, academic integrity, and who decides. On top of that it needs rules for each grade band, a way to vet tools for privacy, a fair process when someone hides AI use, and a named owner who reviews it on a fixed schedule.

This is practical guidance, not legal advice. Your local authority, district or data protection lead has the final word on what applies to your school.

The five parts every policy needs

A policy that only says "use AI responsibly" settles nothing when a dispute comes up. What works is concrete language a teacher can point to.

  1. Purpose and scope. A short paragraph tying AI use to your existing learning goals, and saying who the policy covers.
  2. Definitions. One plain sentence each for "generative AI", "AI-assisted work" and whatever disclosure note you ask for. A ninth grader and a governor should read them the same way.
  3. Acceptable use. Examples at the level of an assignment: "you may use AI to brainstorm topics, not to write the final paragraphs" is enforceable, "use AI ethically" isn't.
  4. Academic integrity. Name AI directly next to your citation and plagiarism rules, which were mostly written before these tools existed.
  5. Decision rights. Who approves exceptions, who signs off on a new classroom tool, and what happens when a rule doesn't fit an assignment.

Rules for each grade band

Supervision and goals differ a lot across a school, and so do the minimum ages the AI companies set themselves. UNESCO's guidance on generative AI in education recommends a minimum age of 13 for using these tools in class. A common shape:

  • In early years and primary, AI is used by the teacher or under full supervision, and anything it produces is checked before it reaches a child.
  • In middle school, students use approved tools for brainstorming, vocabulary or practice questions, with the teacher's permission for that assignment.
  • In high school, students may use AI for drafting help, summaries or revision where the assignment allows it, and always say so.

The disclosure is what holds this together. A short "AI use note" on submitted work does the job: which tool, what it was asked to do, what the student changed afterwards. Put the expectation on the assignment sheet itself, because few students read a separate policy document.

Staff use and vetting tools

Teachers often bring in tools on their own, which is usually good instinct and occasionally a privacy problem. A short checklist before any tool touches student data:

  • How long does the company keep what's uploaded, and does it train its models on it?
  • Is there a written privacy policy and data agreement? In the US, this is where FERPA comes in.
  • What happens if the company has a data breach, and how quickly will it tell you?
  • Is it approved for staff only, or for students too? Keep two lists, because the risks differ.

One rule worth writing in plainly: staff don't upload student work to AI tools through personal accounts. Keep a one-page record for each approved tool (when it was checked, who asked for it, any conditions), so a new AI lead can review the whole list in an afternoon.

Transparency and academic integrity

Disclosure works best as a routine, not as something that only comes up when work looks suspicious. Ask for the AI use note every time AI is allowed, and it becomes normal.

When you suspect undisclosed use, keep the process fair. Look at the work next to any drafts, version history and earlier writing. Talk to the student and ask them to walk you through how they produced it. Then decide, write down why, and offer a way to appeal.

Don't treat an AI detector's score as proof. Detectors make mistakes, and students who write in English as a second language are known to be flagged unfairly. A score can be one signal, never the deciding one.

Clear labels on each assignment prevent most disputes before they start:

LabelWhat it means
AI-freeNo AI at any stage
AI-assisted, disclosedAI allowed for the stages named, AI use note required
AI-supported researchAI allowed for summarising sources, citations still required

Governance and rollout

A policy without a named owner drifts. Name an AI lead to own the document, involve whoever handles data protection for tool reviews and the safeguarding lead for anything students use, and give governors or the board the final sign-off.

Write it with staff, students and parents rather than for them. Policies students helped shape are easier to teach and to enforce; the University of Pittsburgh has a useful guide on co-creating an AI policy with students. Try it in one year group or department first, fix what doesn't work, then roll it out. Many schools add AI as an appendix to their existing acceptable use policy instead of writing a separate document, which keeps everything in one place staff already check.

Review it at least once a year, and more often while a pilot is running, because the tools change faster than most policy cycles. If you want a worked example to start from, Hampshire's HIAS AI policy guidance includes templates, and the UK Department for Education publishes its position on generative AI in education.
