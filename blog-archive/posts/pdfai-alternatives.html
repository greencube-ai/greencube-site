How to choose the right alternative for your workflow

Work through these questions in order, since each one narrows the field faster than the last.

  1. Decide cloud or local first. If a document would cause a problem if it leaked, a local tool like GreenCube or an offline desktop app removes that risk entirely, while cloud tools trade some of that control for faster setup and occasionally sharper reasoning on very long documents.
  2. Check citation and grounding support. If you need to point to the exact paragraph a claim came from, confirm the tool shows passage-level citations rather than a paraphrased summary.
  3. Check file size and page limits. Some free tiers cap you at a few uploads a day or a page count that excludes long contracts or dissertations.
  4. Test OCR on a scanned page. A tool that handles typed PDFs well can still struggle with a scanned image, so run one scanned document through before committing.
  5. Confirm export and integration options. If you need notes to land in a specific app or format, verify that before relying on the tool for daily work.

Pro Tip: Run the same scanned contract through two or three tools before choosing one. OCR accuracy varies more than most product pages admit.

One red flag worth watching for: a vendor that is vague about where your files go after upload, or one whose privacy policy was last updated years ago. Both are reasons to slow down before uploading anything sensitive.

What "offline" actually means and when it matters

"Offline" and "local" mean your file never leaves your computer: no upload, no server processing, no copy sitting on a vendor's cloud storage. A cloud tool, by contrast, sends your document to a remote server, runs the AI there, and returns an answer, which is fast and often capable but means your content exists somewhere outside your control, at least temporarily.

Before trusting any tool with a sensitive file, check a few things directly with the vendor rather than taking a marketing page at its word:

  • Whether the vendor keeps your uploaded files after the session ends or deletes them automatically.
  • Whether your documents are used to train future models.
  • Whether an account or sign-in is required, and what that sign-in actually unlocks.

Vendor policies on this shift often. Claude's own support documentation on context window limits for paid plans is a good example of how product details and data handling specifics change over time, which is exactly why checking current policy beats relying on an older review.

A local setup like GreenCube sidesteps most of this entirely: chats and files stay on your machine, and a one-time Google or Microsoft sign-in is used only to verify the license, not to sync or upload anything — it uses advanced privacy-preserving AI methods to maintain security. Setup downloads one AI model once, and everything after that runs without an internet connection.

Cloud tools still make sense when you are working with public or non-sensitive material and want the broadest possible reasoning power, since very large cloud models currently handle complex, multistep reasoning across huge documents better than a model that fits on a laptop.

Our take on local versus cloud PDF tools

The real split in this category isn't between "good" and "bad" tools, it's between documents you can afford to upload and documents you can't. A student annotating a public research paper has nothing at stake by using a cloud tool. A freelancer reviewing a client's signed NDA, or a tutor going through a student's personal records, has a lot at stake, and no amount of polished citation formatting changes that.

Cloud tools still win when the task genuinely needs scale: reasoning across a huge multi-document set, or handling unusual formatting a smaller local model trips over. But a surprising amount of everyday PDF work, contracts, study guides, personal notes, doesn't need that scale at all, and treating every task as if it does is how sensitive files end up on servers they never needed to touch. Hardware matters here too: a local tool like GreenCube runs on Windows or Mac, and how quickly it answers depends on your computer and which model you pick, not on the software cutting corners.
