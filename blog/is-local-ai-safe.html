7 Controls to Lock Down Local AI for Privacy Conscious Users

By Hector Gras · 7 October 2026

We checked this
  • Which AI doesn't train on your chats? We checked 9 (October 2026)

Local AI is safe for most everyday use. Running the AI on your own computer removes the biggest privacy risk of cloud tools, because your chats and files never reach someone else's server. But "local" isn't a safety guarantee on its own. What matters more is what the AI is allowed to read and do: an app that only answers questions has little to lose, while an AI agent that can run commands or delete files needs real limits.

What running AI locally fixes, and what it doesn't

With a cloud AI, everything you type is sent to the company and kept under its rules. A local AI keeps it on your disk, which settles the question of where your data lives. It doesn't settle the question of permissions. An AI with too much access is risky wherever it runs.

The real risks

Prompt injection

Hidden instructions inside a file or a web page can try to steer the model. Picture a PDF you ask it to summarise, with a buried line telling it to ignore you and do something else. OWASP puts prompt injection first in its 2025 Top 10 for LLM applications, and it applies to local models as much as cloud ones. In a chat app that only answers, the usual harm is a wrong or strange answer. In an agent that can act, it can be much worse.

Code, commands and plugins

Some local setups let the model run commands, call plugins or execute code so it can do more for you. If that access isn't tightly limited, a malicious file or a buggy plugin can turn a chat into a way of running code on your machine.

Tampered or stripped models

Model files come from the internet. One from an anonymous uploader can be altered, or have its safety training removed on purpose (these are often labelled "abliterated" or "uncensored"), and many downloads give you no easy way to check they weren't changed.

Local servers that web pages can reach

Many local AI tools run a small web server on your PC so a chat window or other apps can talk to the model. If that server doesn't check who is connecting, a web page you visit may be able to send it requests too.

Nobody watching but you

Cloud providers watch for abuse across all their users. On your own PC nobody does that, so you're the early warning. Small models can also be easier to talk out of their own safety rules than the big cloud ones.

7 controls that make local AI safer

  1. Give it the least access it needs: read-only access to the folder it works on, no open terminal, nothing outside that folder.
  2. Keep it contained. A separate user account, a container or a virtual machine stops a misbehaving process from reaching the rest of your system.
  3. Check what's listening. Find out which ports and web interfaces your AI tools open, and require a password or token before anything can connect.
  4. Download models from known sources, such as the model maker's own page or a well-known publisher. Check the checksum when one is given, and skip models advertised with their safety removed.
  5. Keep a log the AI itself can't edit, so you can see what happened if something looks wrong later.
  6. Require a human yes for anything you can't undo, like deleting files, sending messages or paying for things.
  7. Treat outside files and web pages as untrusted. That's where most prompt injection starts, so be most careful when the same AI reads them and can also act.

Before you give any AI tool access to files or commands, ask what the worst thing it could do with that access would be. Then narrow the access until the answer is dull.

How to prevent data leaks with local AI

Running the model on your PC stops the obvious leak, but data can still slip out around it. These are the usual gaps:

  • Online features. Many local apps have an optional web search or cloud sync. Keep them off when you work on something sensitive, and check what each switch actually sends.
  • Synced folders. If your chats or outputs are saved inside OneDrive, Dropbox or Google Drive, they get uploaded anyway. Keep sensitive work in a folder that doesn't sync.
  • Copy and paste. The leak is often the next step: pasting the AI's answer, or the original file, into a cloud tool or a browser extension.
  • The disk itself. Turn on BitLocker or Device encryption so a lost laptop doesn't hand over everything on it.

A simple test: switch the Wi-Fi off and do the task. If the app still works, the AI part isn't sending your text anywhere.

A quick way to decide

If the input is yours and any mistake is easy to undo, like summarising your own notes or drafting an email you'll read before sending, local AI is a safe bet. If the input is untrusted (random web pages, files from strangers) or the action can't be taken back, slow down and put a human check in first.

Over time, keep your AI apps updated the way you update your browser, and look again after big updates. Walk away from any tool that can't tell you what it's allowed to do. For the hands-on side, see our offline AI security guide and how to secure AI on your PC.

Questions

What is the safest AI to use?

There isn't one, because safety depends on what a tool can read and do as much as where it runs. An app that only answers questions has little to lose; an agent that can run commands or delete files needs tight limits and your yes before anything you can't undo.

Should I use a local AI?

Yes, if you want your chats and documents to stay on your computer and your tasks are things like drafting, summarising or studying. For the hardest problems or today's news, a cloud AI is stronger.

Is local AI harmful to the environment?

It uses your own electricity while it runs, instead of a data centre's. The models themselves were trained in data centres either way.

Is local AI free?

Some are: LM Studio, Ollama, Jan and GPT4All cost nothing, and GreenCube is €9.99 (US$9.99) once with no subscription. Either way you pay a little for electricity while it runs.

What are the biggest risks of running AI locally?

Prompt injection from files or web pages, apps that let the model run code or plugins without tight limits, tampered model downloads, and local servers that web pages can reach. OWASP ranks prompt injection first in its Top 10 for LLM apps, and on your own PC nobody else is watching for abuse.
