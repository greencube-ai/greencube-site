How Local AI Works on Your Laptop: Run Models with 2–5GB Free

By Hector Gras · 2 September 2026

Local AI works by keeping the model, a file of a few gigabytes, on your computer and running it there with a program called a runtime. When you type, the runtime splits your words into tokens, pushes them through the model on your processor or graphics card, and predicts the reply one token at a time. No server is involved, which is why it works offline and why your chats stay on your machine.

It fits on an ordinary laptop thanks to two things: small models built for home hardware, and quantization, which compresses them. Below is each piece in plain terms, what limits speed, and six steps to get a model running.

The three parts

Every local AI setup has a model, a runtime and an app.

The model is a file, usually 2 to 5 GB for the kind that runs on a laptop. It holds billions of numbers (weights) learned during training. On its own it does nothing.

The runtime loads that file into memory and does the maths. The best-known open one is llama.cpp, which many desktop apps use under the hood.

The app is the part you see: a chat window, a way to download models, settings. Some apps let you pick models from a long list; others choose one for you.

What happens when you press Enter

  1. Your text is split into tokens, the chunks of words the model knows. In English a token is often about three-quarters of a word.
  2. The runtime passes those tokens through the model's layers, which gives a probability for every possible next token.
  3. It picks one, adds it to the text and repeats. That's why answers appear word by word.
  4. It keeps a working memory of the conversation so far (the KV cache) so it doesn't redo the whole chat for each new token. That memory grows as the chat gets longer.

Some apps also look up passages from your own documents first and add them to the prompt, a technique called retrieval-augmented generation, or RAG. Here's how RAG compares with fine-tuning.

Why a model fits on a laptop

The biggest cloud models are far too large for a home computer. Local AI uses small language models, typically 1 to 8 billion parameters, that are designed to be useful at that size.

Quantization then shrinks them further. Models are trained with each number stored in 16 bits, and quantization rounds them to around 4 or 8 bits. A 4-bit model is roughly a quarter of the size and runs faster. The quality loss is small enough that most people don't notice it in everyday chat and summaries, though it can show on harder reasoning. More in LLM quantization explained.

Models for llama.cpp-based apps come as a single GGUF file holding the weights and what the runtime needs to use them. You'll also see safetensors, a format common on Hugging Face and mostly used by Python tools.

What limits speed: memory

To write each token, the runtime has to read essentially the whole model from memory. So two things decide what you can run and how fast it goes.

How much memory you have decides which models fit. As a rough guide, 8 GB of RAM runs small models, 16 GB runs mid-size ones (around 7 to 8 billion parameters), and more opens the door to larger ones.

How fast that memory is decides the speed. A graphics card's own memory (VRAM) is many times faster than normal RAM, which is why a model that fits on the card writes several times faster than the same model on the processor alone. What stays private, and what still goes online

With local AI your prompts and files are processed on your machine, not uploaded. That doesn't always mean nothing touches the internet. Most apps go online to download the model the first time and to check for updates, and some verify a licence. Some have optional features, like web search, that send queries out when you switch them on. Read what each app says it sends.

If you handle health data or client files at work, keeping them on the device lowers the risk, but your legal duties (GDPR, HIPAA or your employer's rules) still apply. Back up and protect the files as you would anyway.

Local or cloud?

Local AI is good at private work: questions about your own documents, drafting, studying, travelling without signal. Cloud AI still wins on the hardest reasoning, on today's news and on very large jobs. The deciding questions are how sensitive your data is, whether you need to work offline, whether you'd rather pay once or every month, and how hard your tasks are. There's more in offline AI vs cloud AI.

It isn't free to run, either. You pay in electricity while it works, in disk space for the model, and in updates you install yourself. A model update usually means downloading a whole new file rather than a small patch.

Getting a model running: six steps

  1. Check your PC: how much RAM it has, whether there's a graphics card and how much memory that has, and 2 to 5 GB of free disk space for a starter model.
  2. Install a local AI app. LM Studio, Jan, Ollama and GPT4All are free; GreenCube is paid.
  3. Pick a model that fits. On 8 GB of RAM, start with a small one (around 2 to 4 billion parameters) in a 4-bit version.
  4. Download it. This is the step that needs internet.
  5. Ask it something simple to check it works, then switch the Wi-Fi off and ask again.
  6. If it's painfully slow, choose a smaller model, close other apps, and check that the app is using your graphics card if you have one.

Questions

Is local AI as good as ChatGPT?

Not on the hardest problems: the big cloud models are clearly stronger at long maths, tricky code and niche facts. For everyday drafting, summarising and questions about your own documents, a good local model holds up well.

How does local AI work without internet?

Once the model file is on your drive, everything (splitting your prompt into tokens, running the model, writing the reply) happens on your own processor or graphics card, so no connection is needed.

Is it worth running AI locally?

Yes, if privacy, working offline or paying once matter more to you than having the most powerful model. If you mainly need top-level reasoning or today's news, a cloud AI is the better fit.

Is local AI free?

Many apps are free, and some are paid once. Running it costs electricity, disk space and a little of your time for downloads and updates.
