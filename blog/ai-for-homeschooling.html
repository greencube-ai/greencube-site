4 Week Privacy First AI Trial for Homeschool Parents

By Hector Gras · 2 October 2026

AI helps a homeschool when it does your prep work and coaches your child. It hurts when it does the thinking for them. Use it for lesson plans, worksheets, quizzes and hints, sit with younger children while they use it, and keep your child's work off servers you don't control. The simplest way to find out if it suits your family is a small four-week trial, laid out below.

I build GreenCube, an AI app that runs offline, so weigh what I say about privacy with that in mind. Most of this post applies to any AI tool.

What AI is good for in a homeschool

The best use is the job that eats your evenings: turning "fractions this week" into a plan, a worksheet and an answer key. An AI drafts that in a minute. You still decide whether it fits your child, but you're editing instead of staring at a blank page.

Things it does well:

  • lesson plans and outlines for a unit
  • worksheets, quizzes and flashcards, with the answer key kept separate
  • hints for a stuck maths problem that stop short of the answer
  • conversation practice in a language you're learning together
  • a short weekly summary of what you covered, for your records

Where it's weak is facts. AI models still get dates, names and numbers wrong, and they sound just as sure when they do. Check anything factual before it goes in front of your child, history most of all.

Ask it to coach, not to answer

The habit that matters most is telling the AI what role to play. Ask it to give one hint at a time, or to reply with a question that points your child to the next step. For writing, ask for two or three specific suggestions instead of a rewrite. The thinking stays with your child, which is the whole point.

For your own prep, tell it the same four things every time: what you want, your child's age or grade, any limits (time, number of questions, reading level) and the format you want back. A vague request gets a vague worksheet.

Safety, privacy and age

UNESCO's guidance on generative AI in education recommends a minimum age of 13 for using these tools in class. At home that translates simply: younger children use AI with you next to them, not on their own.

Privacy is the other half. When you paste your child's essay or a photo of their test into a cloud chatbot, it goes to the company's servers and is kept under that company's rules. A few habits help with any tool:

  • leave out your child's full name, address and anything else that identifies them
  • read the privacy settings, and switch off training on your chats if the tool lets you
  • for real schoolwork (essays, scores, photos of their work), prefer a tool that keeps everything on your own computer

How to choose a tool

Five questions sort out most tools quickly. Where does my child's work go, and is it used to train the AI? Is it a subscription, and what happens to our saved work if we stop paying? Does it cover what we actually teach? Will it run on the computer we have? Can we get our work out of it?

Then try it on something low-stakes, like a lesson plan or a set of practice questions, before you trust it with anything you'll grade or file.

A four-week trial

  1. In the first week, use it alone. Make one lesson plan and one worksheet, check them, and teach with them.
  2. In the second week, let your child use it for hints on maths or reading while you sit beside them. Notice where it helps and where it gets things wrong.
  3. In the third week, give older children more room, with one rule: afterwards they tell you, out loud or in a few lines, what they learned and how the AI helped.
  4. In the fourth week, look back. Keep what worked, drop what didn't, and decide whether it earns a place in your routine.

A month is long enough to get past the novelty and short enough that nothing is lost if you decide against it.

Questions

What is the best AI tool for homeschooling?

There isn't one. Cloud chatbots like ChatGPT and Gemini are the most capable, and offline apps keep your child's work at home. Give two of them the same lesson plan to write and keep the one you'd actually use.

What is the 30% rule for AI?

There's no official 30% rule. The phrase gets used for home-made rules of thumb about how much of a piece of work AI may produce. In your homeschool you set the rule, and "AI gives hints, not answers" is easier to keep than a percentage.

Is there a free AI tool for schools?

Most big chatbots have a free tier, which is fine for drafting lesson ideas. Free tiers come with usage limits and send what you type to the company's servers, so check the privacy settings before using them with your child's work.

What is the school version of ChatGPT?

Some AI companies sell education versions with extra admin controls, usually bought by a school or university rather than a family. For a homeschool the useful question is the same as for any tool: where does your child's work end up?

How much supervision does a child need when using AI?

UNESCO's guidance puts the minimum age for using generative AI in class at 13, so younger children should use it only with an adult beside them. Older children can work more on their own once they know your family's rules.
