The Best AI for Researchers: A Workflow-Based Guide

By Hector Gras · 17 August 2026

The best AI for researchers is a small set of tools, one per stage of the work. Use a search assistant connected to a real scholarly database to find papers, a citation-mapping tool to see how a field fits together, and a general chatbot only for outlining and editing text you'll check. For unpublished data or drafts that shouldn't go to a third-party server, add an assistant that runs on your own computer.

I build GreenCube, one of those local assistants, so weigh my opinion with that in mind. The rest of this post is about the tools that matter more for most research: the ones that show where their claims come from.

What makes an AI tool useful for research

One thing separates research tools from chatbots: whether you can trace each claim back to the sentence it came from. If a tool can't show you the page, treat its answer as a lead, not a fact. Guidance on source-grounded assistants, such as this BYU paper, makes the same point.

The second thing is where the tool searches. An assistant that queries a curated index of papers behaves very differently from one that searches the open web or answers from memory. Clarivate's Web of Science Research Assistant is an example of the first kind: plain-language questions answered from Web of Science's citation database. After that, check that it exports cleanly to Zotero or Mendeley, and whether it has any option for sensitive material.

Finding papers

Discovery tools turn a plain-language question into a search and hand you a ranked shortlist instead of 400 raw hits. Database-connected ones like the Web of Science Research Assistant are the safest bet if your institution has access. Elicit and Consensus are popular AI search tools built on open paper collections, and Semantic Scholar is a free AI-assisted search engine for papers (see Elicit alternatives and Consensus alternatives for more).

They all have blind spots. Paywalled papers are often invisible to them, so a shortlist can look complete while missing the most important paper in your field. Some mix peer-reviewed work with preprints without saying so clearly. Three habits catch most of this: open the original abstract of anything you plan to cite, follow every citation back to the source, and run your key searches in at least two places.

Mapping a literature

Citation-mapping tools such as Connected Papers and ResearchRabbit turn a few seed papers into a network: which papers are cited together, which clusters exist, which authors keep turning up. Early in a review, that shows you whole strands of work you hadn't searched for. Later, it lets you argue for a gap with something more solid than "few studies have looked at this". A map doesn't write the gap statement, but it shows where the gap actually sits.

Pulling data out of papers

Extraction is where AI saves the most hours in a systematic review and where errors hide best. Tools that fill a table with sample sizes, methods and outcomes may quietly work from the abstract when the number you needed was in the full text. Invented numbers that look plausible turn up more often here than in plain summaries. A framework for AI-assisted systematic reviews in Management Review Quarterly found domain-specific models such as PubMedBERT did better than general ones at extraction in biomedicine.

Keep a person checking every number that feeds a conclusion, and spot-check a random sample against the original PDF. Treat an AI-extracted figure the way you'd treat one from a new research assistant in their first week.

Writing and checking before submission

General chatbots like ChatGPT and Claude are good at the language side: outlining a section, condensing your own notes, tightening a paragraph, checking a draft against a submission checklist. They are bad at references. They can produce citations that look right and don't exist, or real papers that don't say what's claimed.

A routine that holds up: draft with help, check every citation against the source yourself, and keep a short log of what the AI touched and how you checked it. Most journals now ask for some disclosure of AI use, so read your target publisher's policy before you submit.

Sensitive data and unpublished work

Most AI research tools process your queries on their servers, and some keep that data. For a search over published papers that hardly matters. For a draft with unpublished results, interview transcripts or anything under an ethics approval, it does, and your institution may have rules about which third-party AI tools are allowed for which kinds of data. Check those first.

A sensible split is to do the public literature work in the cloud tools above and keep sensitive drafts and data on your own machine. Local AI apps run a model on your computer: free ones like LM Studio and Ollama, where you choose and set up the model yourself, or GreenCube, the one I make. Be clear about the limits. A model that runs on a laptop is much weaker than the big cloud models at synthesis across many papers, it has no connection to any scholarly database, and it doesn't know anything published after it was trained. It's useful for summarising your own notes, restructuring a draft or explaining a method, not for finding literature.

Questions

Is ChatGPT Good for Research?

For brainstorming, outlining and editing, yes. For finding literature, no: it isn't connected to licensed scholarly databases and can produce references that don't exist. Use it as a writing aid and check every citation.

What AI Is Better Than ChatGPT for Academic Work?

For finding papers, a search tool built on a scholarly index, such as the Web of Science Research Assistant, Elicit, Consensus or Semantic Scholar, because it answers from real papers you can open.

Which AI Tool Is Best for PhD Research?

A combination: a database-connected search tool for the literature review, a citation-mapping tool to see the field, a reference manager like Zotero, and a local assistant for chapters with unpublished results if your data rules require it.

Which GPT Model Is Best for Research Tasks?

The model matters less than whether the tool searches real sources and shows them. In some fields, domain-specific models such as PubMedBERT do better at extraction than general ones.

Can AI Replace a Systematic Literature Review Process?

No. It can speed up searching, screening and extraction, but a defensible review still needs people checking sources and following the method.
