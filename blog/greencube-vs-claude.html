GreenCube vs Claude: How Local and Cloud AI Actually Differ

The core split between GreenCube and Claude comes down to where the computing happens. GreenCube runs entirely on your Windows PC using a downloaded model, so the processing happens on your own hardware. Claude runs on remote servers, which means every message travels over the internet to reach a much larger model than any laptop could realistically run.

That architecture difference cascades into everything else. Cloud models like Claude tend to deliver stronger long-form reasoning and coding performance, since they're not limited by what fits in your computer's memory. Local apps trade some of that horsepower for something cloud tools can't offer: your chats stay on your device, and there's no internet requirement once setup is done.

Document and image handling is where the trade-off gets specific. GreenCube's All-rounder model can read images and build study guides from documents, but its Quick model is text-only and can't process images at all. Claude handles both text and images natively across its tiers, plus browser and file integrations that a local app simply doesn't attempt to replicate.

A few practical differences worth weighing before you pick a lane:

  • Internet dependency: GreenCube works on a plane, in a basement office, or during an outage. Claude does not.
  • Response latency: local inference speed depends on your hardware, not a server queue, but a cloud model with data center GPUs usually responds faster on complex prompts.
  • Reasoning depth: Claude's larger models handle multi-step analysis and long documents with more consistency than a 3B or 4B parameter model running locally.
  • Data exposure: local chat processing never transmits your conversation content; cloud chat processing does, by design.

Pro Tip: If you're not sure which category fits your habits, track a week of your own AI usage. If most of it is drafting, summarizing, or asking questions about your own files, a local tool covers it. If you're regularly asking for deep research synthesis or production-grade code, that's where cloud reasoning still pulls ahead.

Is GreenCube Really Private? What Data Actually Leaves Your Device

GreenCube's chat and document processing happens locally, and that's the specific claim worth verifying rather than taking on faith. The model runs on your machine, so the text you type and the documents you upload never get transmitted anywhere for analysis.

Two things do leave your device, and it's worth being upfront about both. Signing in through Google or Microsoft is required once, but only to verify your one-time license, not to sync your conversations. Checkout runs through Stripe, which means payment details go through a standard payment processor the same way they would for any other software purchase. Neither step touches your chat content or your documents.

Here's how to confirm this for yourself rather than take a brand's word for it:

  1. Open your system's network monitor while chatting with GreenCube and confirm no outbound traffic spikes during a conversation.
  2. Check that the downloaded model file (roughly 2GB or 4.2GB depending on which you picked) sits in local storage, not a temp cache tied to a server call.
  3. Review the app's permissions in Windows settings to see what it's actually allowed to access.

Be cautious of any AI app, GreenCube included, that adds an optional cloud feature later. Hybrid features (like a cloud fallback for harder queries) change the privacy math the moment they're turned on, so always check what a toggle actually does before enabling it. Partner guides on on-device privacy verification walk through similar checks for other private AI apps if you want a second methodology.

Pro Tip: Turn off your Wi-Fi entirely for five minutes and keep chatting. If GreenCube keeps responding normally, that's a direct, no-guesswork confirmation the chat itself needs no connection.

Which Model and Hardware Do You Actually Need?

GreenCube offers two models at setup, and the choice affects both speed and what the app can do for you. Quick uses Llama 3.2 3B, downloads at around 2GB, and handles text fast but can't read images. All-rounder uses Gemma 4 E4B, downloads at around 4.2GB, reads images, and builds study guides and documents, at the cost of slower responses.

Hardware matters more with the bigger model. All-rounder needs 8GB of RAM at minimum, and that's a floor, not a comfort zone. On an older laptop with 8GB total, expect to notice the fans working and responses lagging, especially with other programs open.

A quick reality check on numbers: a 3B parameter model at 2GB is small enough to run smoothly on nearly any Windows 10 or 11 machine from the last several years. A 4.2GB model asks more of your processor and memory simultaneously, which is why the RAM guidance matters.

Use this quick checklist to decide:

  • Need speed for quick text tasks like drafting emails or brainstorming? Pick Quick.
  • Need to analyze scanned PDFs, photos, or build study guides from images? Pick All-rounder, and confirm you have at least 8GB RAM before installing.
  • Working on an older laptop with limited storage? Quick's smaller footprint is the safer bet.
  • Have a modern machine with 16GB or more? All-rounder runs noticeably better with the extra headroom.

Local-First or Cloud-First: How to Pick the Right AI for You

Three questions cut through most of the confusion. Do you need this to work without internet? Are you handling sensitive documents you don't want touched by a third-party server? Do you need top-tier reasoning for coding, research, or long-form analysis on a regular basis?

If the first two questions dominate your answer, local-first wins. If the third one is your daily reality, cloud-first still has the edge.

  1. The student with sensitive coursework and spotty campus Wi-Fi: local-first. Offline access and private document handling matter more than frontier-level reasoning for essay feedback.
  2. The freelance writer drafting client work on a laptop in cafes: local-first, especially with client confidentiality concerns and no guaranteed connection.
  3. The developer debugging complex, multi-file codebases daily: cloud-first, since raw reasoning depth at that scale still favors larger cloud models.

A few more markers worth checking:

  • Handle medical, legal, or financial documents regularly? Local-first reduces exposure by keeping analysis on-device.
  • Need plugin integrations across dozens of third-party tools? Cloud-first assistants generally have broader ecosystems.
  • Working somewhere with unreliable internet? Local-first is the only one that keeps working.
