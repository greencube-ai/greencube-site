LLM quantization converts a model's weights and, often, its activations from 16-bit floating point into lower-bit formats like INT8 or INT4, cutting memory and speeding up inference. If your hardware is the bottleneck, weight-only 4-bit quantization with GPTQ or AWQ is the safest starting point. If you have FP8-capable hardware and want accuracy closest to the original model, W8A8-FP is effectively lossless and should be your first try.

TL;DR:

  • Using W8A8-FP quantization on hardware supporting FP8 yields near-lossless accuracy, especially on models above 30 billion parameters.
  • For 4-bit weight-only quantization, GPTQ and AWQ are the most reliable options, with AWQ preferred for quick calibration.
  • High outlier channels in activations are best managed through group-wise scaling or non-uniform schemes like NF4 to prevent accuracy loss.
  • Calibration data must match the deployment domain closely, with a few hundred representative samples often sufficient for smaller models.
  • Hardware support determines viable formats: FP8 formats need native FP8 tensor cores, while INT8 and INT4 depend on specific GPU architectures.

The math behind scale, zero-point, and why outliers matter

Quantization maps a continuous range of floating-point values onto a small set of integers using an affine transform: q = round(x / scale + zero_point), and dequantization reverses it with x ≈ scale * (q - zero_point). The scale sets how much real-number range each integer step covers, and the zero-point shifts the range so it can represent asymmetric distributions, like activations that skew heavily positive after a ReLU or GELU.

Symmetric quantization fixes the zero-point at zero and is simpler and faster on most kernels, but it wastes range when the underlying values are not centered. Asymmetric quantization adds a zero-point offset and represents skewed distributions more precisely, at the cost of a slightly more complex dequantization step. Weight distributions in transformers are close to symmetric, so symmetric quantization usually works for weights; activations are often asymmetric, which is one reason activation quantization is harder.

Granularity decides how many values share one scale factor. Per-tensor quantization uses a single scale for an entire weight matrix, which is fast but blunt. Per-channel quantization assigns a scale to each output channel, tracking variation across neurons. Per-group quantization splits a channel into small blocks, commonly 64 or 128 elements, each with its own scale, giving much finer resolution at a modest metadata cost. Smaller group sizes generally preserve more accuracy but add overhead, which is why 128 has become a common default for 4-bit weight-only formats.

The hardest problem in practice is outliers. A handful of activation channels in large transformers can be an order of magnitude larger than the rest, and a naive uniform quantizer will either clip those values or waste most of its resolution accommodating them. Non-uniform schemes like NF4 address this by spacing quantization levels to match the actual distribution of weights (roughly Gaussian) rather than spreading them evenly, so more levels fall where values actually cluster. Group-wise scaling and dedicating higher precision to a small number of outlier channels are the two tactics that most consistently separate a working 4-bit deployment from a broken one.

Choosing between post-training quantization and QAT

Post-training quantization (PTQ) takes a trained model and quantizes it directly, typically using a small calibration set and no gradient updates, which means it runs in minutes to a few hours on a single GPU. Quantization-aware training (QAT) inserts fake quantization operations during training or fine-tuning so the model learns to compensate for quantization error, which requires a training pipeline, labeled or representative data at scale, and substantially more compute and wall time.

For most real-world LLM deployments, PTQ is the right call. It is cheap, fast to iterate on, and, as broad benchmarking has shown, well-tuned PTQ formats often come within a few percentage points of full-precision accuracy. QAT earns its cost when you are targeting extremely aggressive bitwidths (sub-4-bit), when the task is unusually sensitive to precision loss, or when you already have a fine-tuning pipeline running and can fold quantization awareness into it cheaply.

A practical middle ground is a hybrid flow: fine-tune a small adapter with QLoRA on top of a quantized base model, then apply PTQ to the final merged weights before deployment. This captures most of QAT's task-specific recovery without the cost of a full quantization-aware training run, and it fits naturally into workflows that already use LoRA-style adapters for domain adaptation.

Comparing GPTQ, AWQ, SmoothQuant, and other leading PTQ methods

The major PTQ algorithms differ mainly in how they compensate for quantization error and which part of the model they target. A useful way to group them, confirmed by a broad taxonomy of PTQ approaches, is by mechanism: compensation-based, salience-based, migration-based, rotation-based, and optimization-based.

  • GPTQ is compensation-based: it quantizes weights layer by layer and adjusts remaining weights to offset the error introduced by each rounding step, using second-order (Hessian) information. It is broadly robust across different model architectures and remains a dependable default for weight-only 4-bit quantization.
  • AWQ is salience-based: it identifies which weight channels matter most for accuracy by observing activation magnitudes, then protects those channels with finer scaling instead of touching activations directly. It tends to be faster to run than GPTQ and works well when calibration time is limited.
  • SmoothQuant is migration-based: it shifts the quantization difficulty from activations to weights by rescaling both, which lets you quantize activations to INT8 without the usual accuracy collapse. The original SmoothQuant paper shows this migration formula lets large models hit W8A8 with accuracy close to FP16, and the method is already integrated into runtimes like FasterTransformer.
  • Rotation-based methods (QuIP, QuaRot) apply a mathematical rotation to weights and activations before quantization, spreading outlier energy across dimensions instead of letting it concentrate in a few channels. Benchmarking across PTQ method classes found rotation-based techniques hold up best at extreme low-bit settings, where compensation-only methods start to break down.
  • OmniQuant is optimization-based: it learns quantization parameters (clipping ranges, scales) through a lightweight gradient-based search rather than fixing them with heuristics, trading a bit more setup time for better accuracy at aggressive bitwidths.
  • FP8 and W8A8 formats skip the scale/zero-point machinery for activations almost entirely on hardware with native FP8 support, since floating-point formats handle dynamic range more gracefully than integers.

On rule-of-thumb selection: for models above roughly 30 billion parameters running on FP8-capable hardware, W8A8-FP is the strongest general default. For memory-constrained local or edge inference, GPTQ or AWQ at 4-bit weight-only is the standard choice, with AWQ often preferred when calibration time is short. For sub-4-bit targets or unusually outlier-heavy models, combining a rotation-based transform with GPTQ-style compensation, an approach shown to beat single-method quantization at extreme low-bit settings, is worth the added complexity.

Building calibration sets that actually preserve accuracy

Calibration data quality matters more than volume. A 2025 evaluation of quantization techniques found that domain-specific calibration, using real chat logs or code snippets that resemble deployment traffic, preserves task accuracy far better than random token sampling, especially for coding and math tasks where a single wrong token can cascade into a wrong answer.

Building a good calibration set starts with matching the deployment context: pull examples from the same distribution the model will see in production, tokenized the same way, with context lengths that span your expected range rather than clustering at one length. A chat assistant should be calibrated on multi-turn conversational data; a coding assistant should be calibrated on actual code, ideally in the languages it will be used for.

Sample size matters less than composition. A few hundred well-chosen, representative sequences (a common range is 128 to 512 samples) is often enough for weight-only 4-bit quantization on smaller models. Larger, instruction-tuned models pushed toward very low bitwidths need calibration data with more diversity and less repetition, since random tokens increasingly fail to represent the sharper activation distributions found in bigger models. Treat quantization as lossy compression: the calibration set's job is to show the quantizer which information it cannot afford to lose.

Matching bitwidth and hardware to your workload

The right bitwidth depends on whether your workload is latency-sensitive (synchronous, one request at a time) or throughput-sensitive (asynchronous, batched serving). Large-scale benchmarking across the Llama 3.1 family on A6000, A100, and H100 GPUs found that W8A8-FP is close to lossless while W4A16 is the most cost-efficient format for synchronous serving, whereas W8A8 dominates asynchronous, high-throughput scenarios.

  • W8A8-FP (FP8 weights and activations) gives accuracy close to full precision on hardware that supports native FP8 compute, such as Hopper and Ada Lovelace GPUs.
  • W8A8-INT (INT8 weights and activations) runs on a wider range of hardware, including Ampere, but the same benchmarking found it costs roughly 1 to 3 percent average accuracy compared to full precision.
  • W4A16 (4-bit weights, 16-bit activations) rivals 8-bit accuracy in many cases while cutting memory further, making it the strongest choice for latency-bound, single-request serving.
  • INT4 end-to-end compute can unlock large latency gains on hardware that supports it, but decoder-only models show larger perplexity increases at 4-bit than encoder models, so mixed-precision fallbacks for sensitive layers are often necessary.

Hardware generation drives which kernels are even available: Ampere GPUs support efficient INT8 but lack native FP8 tensor cores, Hopper and Ada Lovelace add FP8 support that makes W8A8-FP practical, and older architectures may only have solid INT8 or INT4 kernel support through libraries like AutoGPTQ. Check kernel availability before committing to a format, since the best format on paper is worthless without a fast kernel for it.

KV cache memory grows with context length and batch size, and at long context it can rival or exceed weight memory, so quantizing the KV cache (typically to INT8) is often the highest-leverage change for throughput-bound serving. Reserve cache quantization for scenarios where you have already validated accuracy on your weight and activation format, since stacking multiple lossy compressions compounds error.

A step-by-step PTQ workflow you can run this week

Before touching a quantizer, confirm three things: which base checkpoint and precision you are starting from, which target metric defines "good enough" for your task, and what hardware the quantized model will actually run on. Skipping this step is the most common reason teams re-run quantization from scratch.

  1. Export the model in its original FP16 or BF16 precision and confirm it reproduces published benchmark numbers on a small held-out set.
  2. Pick a PTQ method based on your bitwidth target and hardware, GPTQ or AWQ for 4-bit weight-only, SmoothQuant or FP8 for 8-bit activation quantization.
  3. Assemble a calibration set that mirrors your deployment traffic, in the range of a few hundred sequences for most weight-only setups.
  4. Run the quantizer using a maintained tool such as AutoGPTQ, GPTQModel, or the llmcompressor library, saving intermediate logs of per-layer error.
  5. Validate with unit tests on known input/output pairs, then run a full benchmark suite covering perplexity and task-specific scores before touching production.
  6. Roll out gradually, serving the quantized model to a small traffic slice behind a runtime like vLLM while monitoring for regressions against the FP16 baseline.

Pro Tip: When a quantized model's outputs diverge from the baseline in ways that are hard to explain, dump per-layer activation statistics for both models and diff them; the layer where the distributions first drift apart is almost always where the calibration set failed to cover a real input pattern.

Catching regressions before they reach production

Perplexity is the fastest sanity check, but it is a blunt instrument that can look fine while specific capabilities degrade. Pair it with text-similarity metrics like BERTScore against the FP16 model's outputs, and with task-specific evaluations such as HumanEval for code or targeted math benchmarks, since these catch capability loss that a single perplexity number hides. Comparative studies across model sizes found that larger quantized models track full-precision word choice and structure closely, while smaller models show more variability even when the underlying meaning holds up, so smaller models deserve extra scrutiny.

The three most common failure causes are poor calibration coverage, unhandled activation outliers, and the general sensitivity of decoder-only architectures to aggressive bitwidths, which shows up as larger perplexity jumps at INT4 than in comparable encoder setups. Regressions usually surface as repetition loops, factual drift, or degraded code correctness rather than outright garbage output, which is why automated scoring matters more than eyeballing a few completions.

When you see a regression, the fix is rarely "start over." Try a finer group size first (128 down to 64), then recalibrate with data closer to the failing use case, then consider mixed precision for the specific layers showing the largest per-layer error, and only switch algorithms or raise the bitwidth if those steps fail.

Why we favor conservative quantization for local deployments

Cloud services can patch a bad model overnight. A local, offline product cannot, so reliability and predictable behavior weigh more heavily than squeezing out the last few points of speed. When we think about which quantization strategy to pair with a given hardware tier, we default to the setting that behaves consistently across the widest range of machines rather than the one that benchmarks best on a single high-end GPU.

That bias toward predictability, not raw capability, is the same philosophy behind offline-first tools generally: fewer surprises matter more than peak performance when the software has to work the first time, on hardware you do not control.

— Hector Gras

FAQ

What is LLM model quantization?

LLM quantization converts a model's weights, and often its activations, from 16-bit floating point into lower-bit formats like INT8 or INT4. This shrinks memory use and speeds up inference, at some cost in accuracy that depends heavily on the method and bitwidth chosen.

What is the best quantization method for LLMs?

There is no single best method: it depends on hardware and workload. Broad benchmarking found W8A8-FP is close to lossless on FP8-capable hardware, while GPTQ or AWQ at 4-bit weight-only remain the standard choice for memory-constrained local inference.

When should you quantize a model?

Quantize when memory, bandwidth, or latency limits are blocking deployment, such as running a large model on a single consumer GPU or reducing serving costs at scale. If you have headroom on all three, quantization adds risk without a clear payoff.

How do I quantize a model?

Export the model at full precision, choose a PTQ method suited to your bitwidth and hardware, build a calibration set that mirrors your real traffic, then run a maintained tool like AutoGPTQ or llmcompressor. Validate with perplexity and task-specific benchmarks before rolling the quantized model into production.
