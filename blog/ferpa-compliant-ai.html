7 Step Offline AI for Teachers That Helps Keep Student Data FERPA Compliant

By Hector Gras · 5 October 2026

No AI tool is "FERPA compliant" on its own: FERPA is a US law about how schools handle education records, and your school or district decides which tools you may use with them. What an AI that runs on your own laptop does is keep student documents on that laptop instead of sending them to another company's servers. With your school's approval, as few identifiers as possible and a human checking every output, that's a sound way for a teacher to summarise notes or draft feedback. The seven steps below are a routine for doing it.

This isn't legal advice. I build GreenCube, one of these offline apps, so weigh my view with that in mind; the routine works the same with any tool that runs locally.

Seven steps for handling student documents on your laptop

Running AI on your own computer changes where the data lives. It doesn't change your responsibilities. Doing the same few things every time keeps a small slip from turning into a real problem.

  1. Check your school or district's policy and get approval, in writing if you can, before using any AI tool with student information. Many schools require new software to be cleared by IT first.
  2. Define the task narrowly. Decide exactly what you need (summarise this paragraph, suggest wording for feedback) instead of handing the AI a whole folder.
  3. Remove names and other identifiers, or replace them with placeholders like "Student A", before you paste anything in.
  4. Use a protected device: an up-to-date operating system, a password on your account, and disk encryption switched on.
  5. Keep the work offline. Run the AI with no connection where you can, and never paste a class roster into a cloud chatbot.
  6. Check every output against the original yourself before you act on it or share it.
  7. Keep a short log of what you ran, why, when and who approved it. A plain text file is enough, and it protects you if questions come up later.

How FERPA thinking applies when you work offline

FERPA protects education records and the personal information in them, and it puts the school in charge of who can see that information and why. The US Department of Education's Student Privacy Policy Office explains the law and has guidance for schools.

Working offline removes one risk, the data leaving your device, and leaves the rest in place. Use student data only for the task you defined. Keep a record of what you did. Don't pass on what you process to anyone outside the approved purpose. And remember that "offline" doesn't mean "approved": your school's policy still decides whether you can use the tool at all.

Outside the US the rules differ. In Europe it's the GDPR (see GDPR and AI), and Canada's privacy commissioners have published a joint resolution on education technology that recommends privacy assessments before schools adopt a tool.

What your laptop needs

An AI app on a laptop runs a model that's already trained; it answers, it doesn't learn from your files. That's why it fits on a normal computer at all. The model is smaller than the ones behind ChatGPT or Gemini, so it's best at bounded jobs like summaries, feedback wording and question generation, and weaker on long, complicated reasoning.

Memory decides what runs, and a graphics card decides how fast. With GreenCube, a Windows laptop with about 8 GB of RAM and no graphics card can do everything, including reading PDFs and photos, just slowly. Here is how fast it writes on the PCs we tested:

Questions

Is there an AI tool that is officially certified FERPA compliant?

No. There's no official FERPA certification for software, because the law covers how schools handle education records. Ask your school or district whether a tool fits their policy, and prefer tools that keep data on your device.

Can I use AI on student records without internet access?

Yes, with an app that runs the AI on your computer: once the model is downloaded, it can summarise or draft with the Wi-Fi off. Whether you may use it on student records is still your school's call.

What is the difference between inference and training in local AI?

Inference is the AI answering with a model that already exists; training is building the model, which takes far more computing power than a laptop has. Local apps, GreenCube included, only do inference, which is why they run on a personal computer.

Do I still need human review if the AI output looks correct?

Yes. AI can be wrong in ways that read perfectly well, so treat every output as a draft, check it against the source, and leave the final decision to a person.

How much RAM do I need to run an image-capable AI model locally?

It depends on the app. GreenCube reads photos and PDFs with about 8 GB of RAM, slowly, and noticeably faster with 16 GB; making images, not just reading them, needs 12 GB.
