AI Prompts for Teachers: Copy, Adapt, and Save Prep Time

By Hector Gras · 12 October 2026

The AI prompts that save teachers the most time are specific ones: they say who the AI should act as, the grade and subject, the goal, the format you want back and any time or length limit. Below are prompts you can copy for lesson planning, quizzes and feedback, differentiation, parent emails and admin. Swap the parts in [brackets] for your own class, and treat everything that comes back as a first draft you check.

How to write a prompt that works

Compare "make a quiz about fractions" with "Act as a 4th grade maths teacher. Write a 5-question quiz on adding fractions with unlike denominators, with an answer key." The second one gives the AI nothing to guess, so you get something closer to usable on the first try.

  • Put the details first: role, grade, subject, objective, format, length.
  • Revise instead of restarting. "Make it shorter", "simpler vocabulary" or "add one worked example" is faster than a new prompt.
  • Ask it to critique itself: "What's the weakest part of this plan, and how would you fix it?"
  • Paste a question or model answer you like so it can match your style.
  • Save the prompts that work in a shared doc with your team. A prompt that worked for one unit usually works for the next with small edits.

Lesson planning prompts

  • "Act as a curriculum planner. Build a 2-week unit overview for [grade] [subject] on [topic or standard], with a daily focus, a quick formative check each week and one final task."
  • "Write a [length]-minute lesson plan for [grade] [subject] on [topic]. Include a warm-up, direct instruction, guided practice, independent practice and a closing question."
  • "Break this objective into four smaller steps a student could complete in order, each with a short check-for-understanding question: [objective]."
  • "Rewrite this lesson plan so each activity names the standard it addresses: [standard codes and plan]."
  • "Compress this 45-minute plan into a 20-minute version for a shortened day, keeping the core objective."
  • "Suggest one way to connect this [subject] lesson on [topic] to what students are learning in [other subject]."

Quiz, rubric and feedback prompts

  • "Write a 10-question multiple-choice quiz on [topic] for [grade], with four options each and an answer key at the end."
  • "Create five short-answer questions on [topic] that ask students to explain their reasoning in two or three sentences."
  • "Write a 3-question exit ticket that checks whether students met today's objective: [objective]."
  • "Build a 4-point rubric for a [grade] [subject] essay on [topic], with clear criteria for each level."
  • "Write a 50 to 80 word feedback comment for a student who [what they did well] and needs to work on [one thing]. Keep it encouraging."
  • "Adjust this assignment for a student who needs extended time and simpler instructions, without changing the learning goal: [assignment]."

Check every answer key yourself. AI keys sometimes don't match the question, especially on multi-step maths and science items.

Engagement and differentiation prompts

  • "Create three versions of this activity: on level, simplified for struggling readers, and an extension for advanced students: [activity]."
  • "Suggest a hands-on project [grade] students could finish in one lesson to show they understand [topic]."
  • "Design a classroom debate on [topic] with two clear sides, discussion questions and a 5-minute prep structure."
  • "Write five sentence frames to help multilingual learners discuss [topic] in class."
  • "Build a 10-word vocabulary list for [topic] with student-friendly definitions."
  • "Translate these instructions into [language] and simplify the sentences for a beginner reader: [instructions]."
  • "Design a 4-station rotation for [topic]: one with technology, one hands-on, one in pairs, one independent."

Parent, student and substitute prompts

  • "Write a short, warm email to a parent about their child's progress in [subject], with one strength, one area to grow and one thing they can do at home."
  • "Draft a respectful email to a parent about missing homework, focused on next steps rather than blame."
  • "Write step-by-step instructions a [grade] student can follow to complete [assignment], with materials and an estimated time."
  • "Create a substitute plan for [subject] with the objective, two no-prep activities and a note on routines and behaviour expectations."
  • "Write a short note to families about [event], with the date, what to bring and one thing to do before then."

Use placeholders such as "Student A" instead of real names when you draft these in a cloud tool, and add the specifics before you send.

Admin prompts

  • "Turn these rough notes into meeting minutes with action items and owners: [notes]."
  • "Organise these notes into a prioritised to-do list for this week: [notes]."
  • "Create a 30-minute staff meeting agenda covering [topic 1], [topic 2] and time for questions."
  • "List the materials I need for this week's lessons from this outline: [outline]."
  • "Summarise these test scores into a short paragraph on overall class performance and two trends worth addressing: [scores without names]."

Double-check any numbers in a summary against your gradebook. AI tools can miscount or misread pasted data.

Chaining prompts together

Prompts work better in sequence than alone. For a new week, run the unit overview prompt, paste its output into the daily lesson prompt, then ask for an exit ticket that matches that lesson. For a marking day, summarise the anonymised scores first, check the numbers, then use the feedback prompt to draft individual comments from the patterns you found.

Student data and privacy

Don't type student names, grades or health details into a cloud AI tool your school hasn't approved. UNESCO's guidance on generative AI in education calls for data protection and human review, and state guidance such as Arizona's warns against uploading students' personal information to tools that haven't been approved. In the US, FERPA is the rule to know.

Questions

What are some good prompt ideas for AI?

Good prompts name the role, the audience, the task, the format and a length, for example "Act as a [grade] [subject] teacher and write a 10-question quiz on [topic] with an answer key." Adding an example of what you like helps even more.

What are some good AI prompts for Teacher's Day?

Try "Write a short, warm thank-you message for a teacher who helped students gain confidence in [subject]" or "Draft a class card thanking our teacher for [something specific they did this year]." The more specific the detail you give, the less generic the message.

What are the top 10 AI prompts?

There's no official list. The ones teachers reuse most cover lesson plans, quizzes, rubrics, exit tickets, parent emails, sub plans, feedback comments, vocabulary lists, differentiated tasks and meeting notes, and this article has prompts for all of them.

What are some common AI prompts?

For teachers: lesson summaries, quiz questions, feedback comments and parent emails, because those eat the most prep time. Most people then refine the first draft by adding grade, tone or length.

Is it safe to use AI for student data and grading?

Only in tools your school has approved, or ones that keep the data on your computer. Use placeholders instead of names, and keep final grades yours: AI can draft feedback, but it shouldn't decide marks.
