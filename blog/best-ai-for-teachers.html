Best AI for teachers in 2026: lesson planning, grading and student privacy

By Hector Gras · 15 August 2026

The best AI for teachers depends on the job. For quick lesson drafts, quizzes and parent emails, a general assistant like ChatGPT or Gemini is fast and has free plans. For student practice, a tutor built for classrooms such as Khanmigo fits better than a general chatbot. For anything with student names, grades or writing in it, use a tool your school has approved, or one that runs on your own computer so the work stays on it.

I build GreenCube, one of the run-on-your-computer options, so weigh my opinion with that in mind. Most of this post applies whichever tool you pick.

What teachers actually need from an AI tool

Feature lists matter less than how a tool fits a school. It has to produce a usable draft in the first fifteen minutes, without training. It has to respect your rubric and your standards rather than inventing its own. And it has to get past the realities nobody puts in the marketing: older school laptops, an IT department that reviews anything touching student data, and a district policy on which AI tools are allowed (see school AI policies).

Price shape matters too. Free plans are fine for occasional use but have caps. Subscriptions make sense if the school pays. A one-time purchase suits a teacher paying out of pocket who wants a fixed cost.

The main options

ChatGPT and Gemini

The fastest way to turn a blank page into a lesson outline, a set of questions or a polite email home. Both are strong general models and both have free plans with limits. They run in the cloud, so keep student names and work out of them unless your school has an approved account with the right data terms. OpenAI sells education versions of ChatGPT to schools and universities; what's on offer for individual teachers changes, so check its site.

Khanmigo

Khan Academy's AI tutor and teaching assistant, built for classrooms rather than adapted to them. It's a better fit than a general chatbot when the goal is student practice, because it's designed to guide students instead of handing them answers.

A local AI on your own computer

Local apps run the AI on the laptop in front of you, so student writing, reports and IEP notes are never uploaded anywhere. Free ones like LM Studio and Ollama work if you're comfortable choosing and setting up a model. GreenCube, the one I make, is a Windows app that costs €9.99 once and sets itself up for your PC. It makes quizzes, flashcards, slide decks (saved as PowerPoint), labelled diagrams and documents, and reads PDFs, Word files and photos, all offline after a one-time sign-in and download.

The catch with any local AI: it's weaker than ChatGPT or Gemini on harder tasks, it doesn't know current events unless you switch on web lookups, and an older laptop makes it slow. On an 8 GB laptop with no graphics card it writes at about reading speed on the PCs we tested; on a 16 GB laptop it's two to three times faster. Use Can my PC run AI? to check yours.

Jobs worth trying this week

Each of these takes under half an hour and gives you something you can use or throw away.

  1. A lesson draft: give the topic, grade, length and what students already know, and ask for a warm-up, main activity and exit ticket. Check it against your standards before using it.
  2. Check-for-understanding questions from a passage you're teaching, at a stated difficulty. Read the answer key closely; ambiguous options slip through.
  3. Three versions of the same worksheet at different reading levels, for a mixed class.
  4. A parent email from a short description of the situation, then add the specifics yourself.
  5. Sub plans a non-specialist could follow, from your weekly objectives.
  6. A first pass at feedback on student writing against your rubric, category by category, which you then review and correct.

Read everything before students see it. Factual errors, odd reading levels and bias turn up more often than you'd expect, especially in science and history. We keep a set of prompts for teachers you can adapt.

Grading with AI

AI can speed up a first pass on written work, but it isn't reliable enough to give final grades. Scores drift between runs, it can be swayed by length or confident wording, and it doesn't know your students. Ask for scores per rubric category with a reason for each, so you can see where it went wrong, and keep the final mark yours. If the work has student names in it, either remove them or use a tool that keeps the work on your machine.

To make it grade the way you do, paste your own rubric and ask it to score against exactly those criteria and nothing else. If a tool can't take your rubric, it isn't grading your assignment.

How to check AI-made lesson content

Read everything before it reaches students. The usual mistakes are confident ones: a wrong date, a made-up quote, a number that sounds right. Check names, dates, figures and quotes against your textbook or a source you trust, and ask the AI to list the claims it made so you can tick them off. For anything it can't back up, cut it or rewrite it yourself.

Questions to ask before you use a tool with student data

  • Does student data leave the device? If so, where does it go, and does the vendor use it to train models?
  • What is the sign-in for, and what does it collect?
  • Does it run on the laptops you actually have?
  • What happens to the data if you stop using it?

In the US, FERPA is the rule your IT department will ask about. With GreenCube, the answer to the first question is no: chats and files stay on the PC, and the sign-in only checks that you paid. It doesn't have any formal certification, so your school's own review still decides.

Questions

Which is the best AI for teachers?

There isn't one winner. ChatGPT or Gemini is quickest for drafts, Khanmigo suits student practice, and a local app is the safer choice for anything with student data.

Is there a version of ChatGPT built for teachers?

OpenAI sells education versions of ChatGPT to schools and universities, and its offers for teachers change, so check its site. Khanmigo is an AI assistant made specifically for teaching.

Which AI model is best at teaching-related tasks?

The big cloud models are best at complex lesson design. For everyday quizzes, worksheets and emails, smaller models are usually good enough, and what matters more is whether the tool fits your school's rules.

Is a local AI tool like GreenCube good for grading papers?

For a first pass against your rubric, with you checking every score, yes, and the papers stay on your laptop. It shouldn't give final grades, and neither should any AI.

Do I need internet access to use an offline AI tool?

Only at the start: GreenCube needs internet once to sign in and download the AI. After that it works with no connection, going online only for updates or if you switch on web lookups.
