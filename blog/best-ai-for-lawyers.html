Best AI for Lawyers: Practical Picks for Every Practice

By Hector Gras · 12 August 2026

For legal research you can cite, the best tools are the ones tied to a legal database: Thomson Reuters CoCounsel (Westlaw and Practical Law), Lexis+ with its Protégé assistant (LexisNexis), and Harvey at larger firms. For everyday drafting and summarising, general assistants such as Microsoft Copilot or ChatGPT Enterprise do the job if your firm's terms allow it. When a matter's documents can't go to any cloud service, a local AI that runs on your own computer is the option, with the catch that it has no legal database behind it. Whatever you pick, you check every citation yourself.

I build GreenCube, one of the local options, so weigh my opinion with that in mind. This post is general information about software, not legal or ethics advice. Your bar's rules, your clients' contracts and your firm's IT people decide what you're allowed to use.

Research tools tied to a legal database

The main problem with AI in legal work is invented authority: a confident answer that cites a case that doesn't exist, or a real case that doesn't say what the AI claims. Courts have sanctioned lawyers for filing exactly that. Tools that answer from a curated legal database, and show you the source behind each claim, lower that risk a lot (Bloomberg Law explains the difference). They don't remove it.

Thomson Reuters CoCounsel

CoCounsel does research and drafting on top of Westlaw and Practical Law, so its answers point back to material you can open and read. It suits firms that already pay for Westlaw. The catch is price and process: it's sold through enterprise contracts, and you get a demo rather than a quick self-serve trial.

Lexis+ with Protégé

The LexisNexis equivalent. Protégé is the AI assistant inside Lexis+, working from LexisNexis content for research, summaries and drafts. If your firm lives in Lexis, start here. Like CoCounsel, pricing is by quote.

Harvey

Harvey is built for large firms and in-house legal teams: contract analysis, due diligence, drafting and research, with the security reviews and admin controls big organisations expect. It's an enterprise purchase, so it rarely makes sense for a solo practice.

General assistants for drafting and summaries

Microsoft Copilot for Microsoft 365 works inside Word, Outlook and Teams. If your firm already runs on Microsoft 365, it's the least disruptive way to get help with emails, first drafts and meeting notes. ChatGPT Enterprise and the business plans of Claude and Gemini are strong general models with business data terms (OpenAI says it doesn't train on Enterprise customers' data). The American Bar Association notes that lawyers use these mainstream models alongside the legal platforms.

None of them is connected to a legal database. They're good at structure and language and unreliable on authority, so treat every case name, statute number and quote as unverified until you've checked it. Use the business plans, not the free consumer apps, for anything involving a client.

What AI is good and bad at in legal work

The useful jobs are the ones where you already have the document and you'll read the result anyway: summarising a long contract or transcript, pulling dates and parties into a list, turning rough notes into a structured memo, drafting a routine letter, rewriting a paragraph in plain words for a client, or listing issues worth checking.

The risky jobs are the ones where the AI supplies the law: finding authority, stating what the current rule is in your jurisdiction, or telling you a clause is enforceable. General models get these wrong in ways that look right. Even grounded tools need a lawyer reading the sources. The responsibility for what goes out under your name stays with you.

Questions to ask before you upload client files

Professional rules on competence and confidentiality mean knowing what a tool does with your data, not just what it writes. Before a cloud tool sees client documents, get clear written answers to these:

  • Where are uploaded documents stored, for how long, and who at the vendor can see them?
  • Does the vendor use your inputs to train or improve its models?
  • Is there a data-processing agreement, and where is the data processed (this matters for EU clients)?
  • Can they send a current SOC 2 Type II or ISO 27001 report, not just a logo on the website?
  • Is there a log of queries and outputs you can retrieve later?
  • Can they show, live in a demo, the source document behind a citation from your own jurisdiction?

If a vendor hesitates on the last one, the grounding is more marketing than feature.

When a local, offline AI makes sense

A local AI runs on your own computer. The documents you give it are read from your disk and never uploaded, and it keeps working with the Wi-Fi off. That fits a narrow but real set of situations: a client contract that forbids cloud processing, a matter so sensitive you'd rather no third party touched it, data that has to stay in one place, or simply working on a train or in a courthouse with no connection.

The trade-off is capability. A model small enough to run on a laptop is much weaker than the cloud models above, especially on long documents and subtle reasoning, and it has no legal database at all. It's a private assistant for reading and drafting, not a research tool. Treat its output the way you'd treat a first-year's rough draft.

Questions

Is Claude or ChatGPT better for lawyers?

Neither is built for law. Both are general models with no legal database, so the deciding factors are your firm's data terms and how well each handles your kind of documents. Try both on a non-confidential matter you know well, and check every citation either way.

Which AI are law firms actually using?

Larger firms tend to buy research tools tied to Westlaw or LexisNexis, such as CoCounsel and Lexis+ with Protege, plus platforms like Harvey, and Copilot where they run Microsoft 365. Smaller firms mix practice-management tools like Clio with general assistants.

Is there a ChatGPT built specifically for legal work?

Not from OpenAI. The closest thing is a legal research tool that answers from a legal database and shows its sources, such as CoCounsel or Lexis+ with Protege.

Which AI is best when client confidentiality rules out cloud tools?

One that runs on your own computer, such as LM Studio or Ollama (free, more setup) or GreenCube (EUR 9.99 once, picks the model for you). They work offline and never upload your files, but they are weaker than cloud AI and have no legal database, so use them for reading and drafting, not research.
