How Do I Choose the Right Local Desktop AI?

Picking a local alternative comes down to three checklists: privacy, usage fit, and purchase terms, as advised by AI Agency Australia for professional buyers considering vendor due diligence. Skip any of them and you risk buying something that doesn't match your machine or your comfort level with data handling.

Privacy checklist

  1. Confirm the license terms state chats and documents stay on your device, not just "encrypted in transit."
  2. Check whether the required sign-in (Google or Microsoft, in GreenCube's case) is used only to verify the license, or whether it also enables background data collection.
  3. Look for explicit language about telemetry: does the app phone home usage data by default, and can you turn that off?

Usage checklist 4. Verify whether the model you'll actually use supports images and documents, or plain text only, since that varies by model tier, not just by app. 5. Match the model size to your hardware before buying. A hybrid workflow using local tools for sensitive work and cloud tools for occasional heavy tasks is a reasonable fallback if your machine is underpowered. 6. Confirm the app genuinely works with no internet connection once the model is downloaded, not just "mostly offline."

Purchase checklist 7. Check the refund window. GreenCube offers 14 days. 8. Confirm it's a true one-time license, not a "one-time fee" that later requires a renewal for updates. 9. Read what the installer actually downloads. A local AI models guide can help you understand what that first download includes before you commit.

Pro Tip: Check your GPU's video memory before picking a model size, not just your system RAM. Running out of VRAM is what actually slows a local AI down, not disk space.

Can a Local AI Fit Into Your Existing Workflow?

Local desktop AI tools scale differently than cloud subscriptions. There's no seat count to negotiate and no usage meter to watch. Once GreenCube's model is downloaded, you can run it as many times a day as you want at no extra cost, whether that's one document a week or twenty.

Integration is more limited than what you'd get from a cloud API. GreenCube and similar local apps are designed as standalone assistants, not as a plug-in layer for existing business software. If your workflow depends on connecting AI output directly into a CRM or a shared team pipeline, a local desktop app is not built for that job.

Where local tools do integrate well is at the individual task level: reading a PDF before a meeting, drafting a document, summarizing a report you can't upload anywhere. Local-first architecture keeps that work self-contained, which is exactly the point for privacy-conscious professionals, even though it means less automation than a networked tool would offer. Community support also looks different here. Generic local AI tools often have active forums and plugin ecosystems built around open runtimes; a focused app like GreenCube trades that breadth for simplicity, with fewer moving parts to configure.

Why VRAM Matters More Than the Marketing Says

Most comparisons of local AI tools spend their time on price and privacy and barely mention the one factor that actually determines whether the thing feels usable: your graphics card's memory. Windows itself documents this plainly. Local inference depends on CPU, GPU, memory, and storage together, and when VRAM runs short, the workload falls back to the CPU and can slow to a crawl.

That's the gap between what a spec sheet promises and what you'll actually experience. A laptop with plenty of RAM but a weak GPU can still choke on a model that looks modest on paper. This is where GreenCube's two-tier approach earns its keep: Quick is genuinely light enough for machines without much graphics power, while All-rounder asks for real memory headroom in return for reading images and building documents. Generic local AI tools that hand you a dozen model choices don't solve this problem for you. They just move the decision earlier and expect you to already know your hardware's limits.

The other overrated idea is that local always means slower. For repetitive document work, local models often finish faster than cloud workflows once you count upload and download time. The right move isn't chasing the biggest model you can find. It's matching model size to the machine in front of you and accepting that a cloud model still wins on the hardest reasoning tasks.
