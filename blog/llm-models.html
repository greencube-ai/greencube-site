Run LLMs Locally: 2–5GB Models for Privacy-First Users

By Hector Gras · 31 August 2026 · updated 10 October 2026

We tested this
  • Gemma 4 requirements: what PC you need to run it locally

An LLM (large language model) is a neural network trained on huge amounts of text to predict the next piece of text. That one skill, done at enormous scale, is what powers ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude and most AI assistants. Some LLMs only run on their makers' servers (GPT, Gemini, Claude); others are "open-weight" models anyone can download and run on their own computer (Llama, Gemma, Qwen, Mistral, DeepSeek, gpt-oss). The small open ones fit in 2–5 GB and run offline on an ordinary PC.

Here's how they work in plain terms, how they're trained, where they fail, and what you need to run one yourself.

How an LLM works

First the text is cut into tokens: whole words, pieces of words or punctuation. In English, 100 tokens is roughly 75 words. Each token becomes a list of numbers (an embedding) that captures something about its meaning.

Those numbers go through a stack of transformer layers. The key step inside each one is attention: every token looks at the others in the text and works out which ones matter for it. Because attention handles the whole sequence at once rather than word by word, transformers train efficiently on many GPUs, which is a big part of why they took over. Position information is added so the model knows that "dog bites man" and "man bites dog" are different.

To answer you, the model predicts the most likely next token, adds it to the text, and repeats, one token at a time. It isn't looking up a stored answer; it's choosing from probabilities at every step. That's why the same question can get slightly different wording each time, and why a model can sound sure about something that's wrong.

Types of LLM

By design, there are three main kinds of transformer:

  • Decoder-only models (GPT, Llama, Gemma and nearly every chatbot) generate text one token at a time.
  • Encoder-decoder models (like T5) read the whole input first, then write the output, which suits translation and summaries.
  • Encoder-only models (like BERT) don't write free text; they're used for classifying text and ranking search results.

By access, there are closed models you use through an app or API, and open-weight models whose trained parameters are published for download. "Open-weight" isn't always fully open source: the training data and code usually stay private, and some licences add conditions on commercial use.

How LLMs are trained

Pretraining comes first. The model reads a vast amount of text from the web, books and code and learns to predict the next token. No one labels the data by hand. This is where it picks up grammar, facts, patterns of reasoning, and the biases in that text.

Fine-tuning turns that raw predictor into an assistant. It's shown many examples of instructions paired with good answers, so it learns to follow directions instead of just continuing the text.

RLHF (reinforcement learning from human feedback) then polishes it. People rank several answers to the same prompt, a reward model learns those preferences, and the LLM is trained toward answers people prefer. This shapes tone and helpfulness a lot. It doesn't make the model truthful.

What LLMs do well, and where they fail

They're good at drafting and editing, summarising, translating, explaining, writing and fixing code, and working through problems step by step. Connected to tools like search or a calculator, they can do more.

The failures are just as real. They make things up (hallucinate) because they're built to produce plausible text, not checked facts; invented citations are a classic. They repeat biases from their training data. They only know what was in that data, so they miss recent events unless they can search. And a text-only model can't read an image; that needs a model trained on images too.

The best defence against made-up facts is to give the model the source: paste the document, or use a tool that feeds it the relevant passages (called retrieval, or RAG). Then check anything that matters.

The main models right now

Among closed models, the best known are OpenAI's GPT models (behind ChatGPT), Google's Gemini and Anthropic's Claude. They're the most capable and run only in the cloud. ChatGPT itself is a product, an app built on OpenAI's LLMs, and LLMs are one kind of generative AI, the kind that works with text.

Among open-weight models: Meta's Llama, Google's Gemma, Alibaba's Qwen, Mistral's models, DeepSeek's models and OpenAI's gpt-oss. Most come in several sizes, from about 1 billion parameters (fine for a phone or laptop) to hundreds of billions (data-centre hardware only). We compare two of the big families in Llama 3 vs Gemma.

Running an LLM on your own computer

Size is the first thing to check. At 16-bit precision, each billion parameters takes about 2 GB. Local apps use quantized files, which store the numbers with about 4 bits instead, roughly a quarter of the size. That's how a model like Gemma 4 E2B fits in a 2.6 GB file and Gemma 4 E4B in 4.2 GB. See LLM quantization for the details.

Questions

What are the different types of LLM models?

Three main kinds: decoder-only models, which generate text like most chatbots; encoder-decoder models, for translation and structured summaries; and encoder-only models, for sorting text into categories rather than writing it.

Is ChatGPT an LLM or generative AI?

Both. ChatGPT is an app built on OpenAI's LLMs, and LLMs are the kind of generative AI that works with text.

What are the top LLM models right now?

Among closed models, OpenAI's GPT models, Google's Gemini and Anthropic's Claude are the best known, and they only run in the cloud. Among open-weight models you can download: Meta's Llama, Google's Gemma, Alibaba's Qwen, Mistral's and DeepSeek's models, and OpenAI's gpt-oss.

What are LLM models, in plain terms?

A neural network trained on huge amounts of text to guess what comes next. Done at enormous scale, that one skill powers chatbots, code assistants and summarising tools.

Can I run an LLM without an internet connection?

Yes, with an open-weight model on your own computer. Small ones are 2 to 5 GB files that run on an ordinary PC; some apps need internet once to download the model or check a licence, then work offline.
